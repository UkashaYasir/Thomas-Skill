Your child probably loves you more than almost anyone on Earth.
And they are still hiding things from you.
Not drugs.
Not secret relationships.
Something much earlier.
Thoughts.
Fears.
Tiny moments you forgot...
that they didn’t.
Because children do not always hide things
because they distrust their parents.
Sometimes they hide them because
they love their parents so much
that disappointing them feels terrifying.
And if children could tell their parents seven things
without worrying about the reaction,
these might be the ones.
Number six may change the way you react tonight.
Number one.
Sometimes I need you to listen,
not fix me.
Your child comes home.
“Today was terrible.”
And instantly your parent brain activates.
Who did it?
What happened?
Did you tell the teacher?
Here’s what you should do tomorrow.
Within thirty seconds,
you have accidentally launched a federal investigation.
There is just one problem.
Your child did not ask for a solution.
They were asking:
“Can you sit inside this feeling with me for a minute?”
Adults do this too.
Imagine telling your partner:
“I had the worst day.”
And they immediately pull out a whiteboard.
“Excellent. I’ve identified four efficiency improvements.”
You would probably throw away the whiteboard.
Children are not always asking us to remove the problem.
Sometimes they are asking us to prove
they do not have to carry it alone.
Number two.
I notice when your phone is more interesting than me.
This one hurts
because most parents are not deliberately ignoring their children.
You are answering a message.
Checking work.
Looking at directions.
Doing something genuinely important.
But your child does not see the reason.
They see your eyes.
They start a story.
You say:
“Uh-huh.”
They keep talking.
“Really?”
But you still have not looked up.
Eventually they stop.
And here is the dangerous part.
They might not complain.
They simply learn:
“This isn’t a good time.”
Then: “Mom’s busy.”
Then years later:
“She wouldn’t understand anyway.”
You do not need hours of perfect attention.
But when your child brings you something small,
remember that to them it may not be small.
The frog they saw at recess today
may be practice for the secret
they need to tell you at fifteen.
Number three.
Your stress becomes my stress.
Children are emotional detectives.
They notice the silence between parents.
The bill on the counter.
The whispered conversation.
The face you make after reading a message.
They notice when Dad sleeps on the couch.
They notice when Mom says she is fine in a voice
that clearly does not mean fine.
And when children notice something but do not understand it,
their imagination fills the gap.
Often with something worse.
“Are we losing the house?”
“Are Mom and Dad separating?”
“Is Grandma dying?”
“Did I do something?”
You do not have to tell children every adult problem.
But silence is not always protection.
Sometimes one simple sentence removes a weight
they never told you they were carrying.
“I’m stressed about money,
but you are safe.”
“Mom and I disagreed,
but we’re working through it.”
“I’m upset today,
and it isn’t because of you.”
That last sentence can matter more than you think.
Because children are very good at noticing tension.
They are not always good at explaining it.
Number four.
Please stop comparing me.
Your brother was reading at your age.
Your sister never behaved like this.
Why can’t you be more like your cousin?
Ah yes.
Cousin Kevin.
Perfect grades.
Perfect manners.
Six-figure job.
Apparently invented sleep.
Parents often think comparison creates motivation.
But imagine your boss saying:
“Why can’t you be more like Sarah?
Sarah is incredible.”
Every.
Single.
Week.
Eventually you do not become Sarah.
You simply start believing Sarah is evidence that something is wrong with you.
Children need standards.
They need encouragement.
But they also need to know
they are not competing for their place in the family.
Your child is not the unfinished version of their sibling.
They are a different person.
Number five.
You forgive yourself faster
than you forgive me.
Dad forgets the permission form.
“Oops.”
Child forgets homework.
“How many times do I have to tell you?”
Mom loses her keys.
“I’m exhausted.”
Child loses a water bottle.
“You need to be more responsible.”
Kids notice this.
Adults receive context.
Children receive correction.
And no, that does not mean children should never be corrected.
It means one of the most powerful things a parent can model is not perfection.
It is recovery.
“I messed that up.”
“I got frustrated.”
“I should have handled that differently.”
“I’m going to try again.”
A child who watches you repair a mistake
learns something much bigger than the mistake itself.
Being wrong does not make you worthless.
Making a mistake does not end the relationship.
And now number six.
I’m scared to disappoint you.
This is deeper than grades.
Your child brings home a test.
Before they show you the score...
they look at your face.
They miss the goal.
They look toward the sideline.
They spill something.
They freeze for half a second
and watch your reaction.
Before your child shows you the grade,
they may already be checking the emotional weather.
Because sometimes the scariest part of failing is not the grade.
It is finding out whether failure changes the way you look at them.
Children become experts at reading approval.
And if love seems brightest after achievement,
a child can begin to wonder
what happens when achievement disappears.
That is when mistakes get hidden.
Grades get hidden.
Problems get hidden.
Not because your child stopped loving you.
Not necessarily because they stopped trusting you.
Sometimes because they desperately want
to remain the version of themselves they think you love most.
Celebrate success.
Absolutely.
Be proud.
But make failure safe enough to tell you about.
Because one day the mistake may be much bigger than a bad test.
And you want your child thinking:
“I need Mom.”
Not:
“I need to make sure Mom never finds out.”
And number seven.
I need to know
you love me most when I’m hardest to love.
This is the one children rarely know how to say.
The slammed door.
The eye roll.
The tantrum.
“I hate you!”
None of those behaviors should simply be accepted.
Boundaries still matter.
Respect still matters.
But underneath some of the ugliest moments of childhood
is a quiet question.
“What happens to us when I’m at my worst?”
Children know they are lovable when they are smiling.
When they are helpful.
When they get the grade.
When they score the goal.
The deeper security comes from discovering:
“I can have a terrible day...
and this relationship survives.”
You can correct behavior without withdrawing love.
“You cannot speak to me like that.”
“We’ll talk when we’re calmer.”
“And I love you.”
All three things can be true.
Maybe that is what children struggle to explain.
They do not need parents who never say no.
They do not need parents who never get angry.
They do not need perfect childhoods.
They need enough moments that tell them:
“I see you.”
“I hear you.”
“You can make mistakes here.”
“You can tell me the truth here.”
“And when things get difficult,
I’m still your parent.”
Years from now, your child may forget the toy you bought.
The perfect birthday.
The expensive vacation.
But they may remember exactly how safe it felt...
to tell you the truth.
And that may be one of the greatest gifts a parent can give.
