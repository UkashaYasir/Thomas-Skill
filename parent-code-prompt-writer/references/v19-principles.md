# Version 19 principles — the law for every frame (read after v24-standard.md; overrides every older file)

> **v24 (9 Oct 2026):** `references/v24-standard.md` — Thomas's final word after Video 08 — wins over this file where they
> differ: the ground is pure white; set pieces are thin black line with white fill; the colour focus is bright and vivid by
> default, with colour chosen by meaning (yellow surprise, red conflict, blue trust, purple phones, green growth, warm
> positive, grey absence); a drawn halo or rainbow burst may make a key object glow; important objects may be two or three
> times larger, and exaggerated cutaways (the helicopter) are allowed; characters never too small to read; zoom plans
> reuse a still; on-screen words are short, playful, hand-lettered, never numbered titles. Everything below that v24 does
> not change still stands.


Source: Thomas's messages of 5 Oct 2026 after "Seven Things Your Child Can't Tell You" (long note, short note, the
summary he approved, his answer on real objects, his final priority list) and Muhammad's direction while turning them
into this skill. Thomas's words below are the client's final word; where any older file in this skill says otherwise,

## 0. How to read this skill now

1. **Thomas's final words are the principles.** Every point he made is implemented, across the whole script — not only
   where he gave an example — and exactly as he said it: no overdoing, no undoing.
2. **No numbers for creative choices.** No counts, quotas, percentages, caps, minimums or "per N seconds" targets for
   ideas, metaphors, close-ups, shot types, locations, characters, props, edits, humour, words, interrupts, colours or
   library entries. Each choice is judged by the moment and by the rules below. Numbers stay only where they are
   technical: hex colours, the 16:9 frame, the reference proportions of the characters, version and file numbers, and the
   voice-over timing Muhammad supplies. Older files that still carry creative numbers are superseded on that point.
3. **The system is open, not a menu.** Every library in this skill (situations, behaviours, interactions, compositions,
   places, props, metaphors, expressions, humour, contrasts, interrupts, characters) is a set of seed examples plus a
   method for making new ones. When a line needs something that is not listed, invent it with the method. After every
   video, add the new entries that worked back into the library so the system keeps growing.
4. **Ideas: explore every direction, keep the strongest.** For every line, think through the real everyday moment, the
   behaviour, a reaction, an object, a contrast, humour, a metaphor and an unexpected angle — and keep going until one idea
   is clearly the strongest. Pick it. Record the runner-ups so later lines can use them and nothing repeats.
5. **Emotion before decoration.** "For every scene, ask: What is the viewer supposed to feel here? Only after that should we
   decide: background, prop, color, camera angle, movement."
6. **Plan before production.** "Define the emotional beats, camera angles, reactions, and visual contrasts very clearly
   before production starts." Every video gets a shot plan (references/shot-plan-v19.md) before any prompt is written.
7. **Better, not more.** "I do not simply want 'more images.' I want better and more varied images with a clear purpose."
   With 300+ images, "the viewer should still never feel like the visuals are repeating or that the same composition keeps
   coming back."
8. **Less, but stronger.** Less: background, decoration, full-screen colour, random props, generic symbols. More: emotion,
   facial expressions, gestures, relatable situations, contrast, close-ups, meaningful colour, strong visual ideas,
   humour, memorable moments.
9. **The style stays fully illustrated and consistent.** Real-photo objects are out: "let's leave real objects out… keep
   refining and strengthening our own visual language." The goal is a style that is "unique, recognizable" and consistent
   video after video.

## 1. Background and colour

- "White or very light neutral backgrounds as the default." Default ground: very light neutral; pure white for face-only
  frames, object-only frames, word frames and deliberate white breaks.
- "Only the important object or emotional element should carry strong color." Each frame names its one colour element
  (an object, part of an object, or nothing when the face carries the frame). "If the phone is important, let the phone
  stand out. If the face is important, keep almost everything else neutral."
- "Background environments can often be shown only with simple outlines"; "do not fully color every object just because it
  exists in the scene." Places, furniture and secondary objects are thin soft-grey outlines with white fill — complete,
  closed line drawings, never coloured rooms, tinted walls or coloured furniture.
- Full-colour scenes stay only "for emotional peaks and night scenes" (the summary Thomas approved), and they stay rare —
  "full-screen color" is on his "Less" list. A full-colour field never sits behind a face close-up (the face is important
  there, so everything else stays neutral).
- Characters must still be seen first: white characters with bold black outlines on the very light neutral ground, set
  pieces in thinner, softer lines that never compete, nothing white-filled overlapping a character's outline.
- Scenes in the past sit on a faded light-grey ground (MEMORY) — light and neutral, so close-ups inside a memory may stay on it.
- Red stays for danger or a warning only.

## 2. Camera and composition

- "Bring the camera closer": "close-ups, extreme close-ups, face-only moments, face + hands, emotional details." "Sometimes a
  large face on a white background is much stronger than a complete room." "Important emotional lines should visually feel
  important."
- Close-ups must make sense (Muhammad): every close-up has a reason (for example the emotional peak of the line, a reaction,
  or a detail the line names); the viewer knows where we are (a wider frame of the place came first) and who looks at whom (the
  gaze matches the person or thing established before — eyeline match); the face reacts to something visible or clearly
  placed (hands, the object, the other person's shoulder, the other face in the next frame); the head shape stays whole and
  readable with open space on the side the eyes look (lead room) — eyes-only frames are the one planned exception, with the
  cause shown; no random crops.
- "More variation in camera distance": wide, medium, close-up, extreme close-up, object close-up, reaction shot, and face +
  hands. "Avoid staying too long in the same medium/wide framing." The same character in back-to-back frames changes size
  or angle clearly (small changes read as mistakes). Inside one scene characters keep their screen sides (180° rule);
  variety of sides and layouts comes between scenes. No composition comes back unchanged.
- Natural camera positions; exaggerated angles render badly.

## 3. Faces, bodies and interaction

- "Stronger facial expressions… eyes, eyebrows, mouth movement, head position, hand gestures, posture, eye direction. The
  characters should not just 'stand in the scene.' They should clearly react." Every performance names the seven features the shot can show (in WIDE and MEDWIDE the body carries the feeling and each small face keeps one bold shape; eyes-only frames use the eyes, eyebrows and eye direction), as one
  clear emotion described by what the face and body do (not a label), with a verb, never a static state.
- "More real interaction between characters": every frame with more than one person shows an action and a visible reaction.
  Emotion also shows through "eye contact, hesitation, body language, distance between people, hands, reactions,
  silence." Distance is planned: apart for conflict, close for repair, touch where it pays off.
- Love, trust, attention and emotional safety are shown through behaviour: "a parent quietly sitting next to a teenager; a
  teen looking away but still listening; a hand reaching out; a parent stopping before reacting; a child hesitating before
  telling the truth; someone noticing the other person is uncomfortable; a parent kneeling down to the child's level; two
  people sitting together without needing a heart symbol."
- Muhammad: interaction must grow, and the cast must grow with it — any character a story needs, designed by the method in
  references/cast-design-v19.md (never bald or a featureless round head; distinct hair outline; copied from the MOM or SON
  reference).

## 4. Story content

- "More real everyday parenting situations… moments that parents and teenagers immediately recognize": the teen's "I'm fine"
  that clearly isn't; too many questions; the eye roll; hiding something; the parent who walks past, notices, comes back; the
  teen who wants attention but pretends not to care; silence misread as disrespect; starting to say something, then
  changing your mind; help that makes it worse; awkward silence after an argument — and every other moment like these the
  script calls for.
- "Stronger visual metaphors": a metaphor is used where it adds meaning the words don't already carry; real moments carry
  the everyday lines. Not every line needs a metaphor.
- "Better and more mature props"; "we are not creating a kids channel." No simple or childish symbols (the heart phone is his
  example): no hearts, stars, trophies, emoji-style icons or picture bubbles standing in for a feeling.
- "Every recurring object should have a reason, evolve with the story, mean something different later, support the
  emotional progression. It should not return only because it looks good." Each return also gets a new picture.
- "Fewer objects, stronger meaning": "one strong object, one strong face, one strong gesture." "The viewer should always
  know immediately where to look." "Reduce full room scenes… one door outline, one bed outline, one chair, one object is
  enough."

## 5. Humour, words, contrast, interrupts

- Humour: "not childish humor… exaggerated parent reaction; teenager giving a 'seriously?' look; awkward pause; parent
  overthinking something simple; visual contradiction between what someone says and what they actually do; short funny
  reaction before returning to the serious topic." Built as setup, a held beat, then the reaction; the picture can
  contradict the fixed voice-over line.
- Words: "a few strong on-screen words or short phrases… only at strong moments, not constantly" (TRUST, SAFE, LISTEN,
  TESTING, SHAME, CONTROL, MISREAD, ATTENTION, OVERWHELMED, HONESTY, NOT REJECTION). Idea words from the line, never a full
  sentence, on the spoken word, in one consistent style, added by Muhammad in Premiere.
- Contrast inside and across frames: "empty background vs strong object; neutral scene vs one bright color; wide shot vs
  extreme close-up; serious moment vs humorous reaction; still moment vs movement; small prop vs large face; silence vs
  strong visual beat."
- Pattern interrupts: "suddenly white background; extreme close-up; one-word text; unexpected object; funny reaction;
  strong pose; short metaphor; sudden silence visually; large facial reaction." The video never settles into one look; pace
  follows the emotion — more switches through calm explanation, held related frames at the peaks.

## 6. Production constraints that stay in force

- Only two reference images exist, MOM and SON; every other character is drawn by copying one of them and changing only
  hair, size/age and small accessories where needed, described in the prompt.
- Script lines and voice-over timing are final from Muhammad; the visuals serve them and never change them.
- In-scene changes are image-edit prompts on the generated frame (detailed, one change at a time), and objects appear with
  flat-shape mask reveals — so revealed props sit apart from hands and bodies. Important moments can be told as a short
  sequence of stills in one scene (not every scene).
- Muhammad generates from the Copy page only, in Flow, and edits in Premiere; the whole video is produced in one pass, so
  the plan and prompts must be complete in one delivery.
- Characters: no clothing, minimal accessories, the references' proportions, strong readable faces, no teeth.
