### Strongest 10 (read this first)
1. **Emotion decides the frame.** Murch weights emotion above story, rhythm and composition combined [S1]. Write "viewer feels:" before choosing size, colour or background.
2. **Shot changes must be big.** Cuts "neither subtle nor total" feel like mistakes [S3]. Same-character frames jump ≥2 steps on the size ladder, and medium→medium runs fail QA.
3. **ECU = bold type** [S2]. Ration it for peak lines and cut to the reaction, not the illustration: the cut gives the face its meaning (Kuleshov) [S4].
4. **Contrast raises intensity; affinity rests it** [S6]. Plan contrast per chapter instead of spreading it evenly.
5. **Characters do, then react.** Emotion leads to action; acting is reacting; a scene is a negotiation [S7]. Silhouette-clear, asymmetric poses with one dominant gesture [S8][S9]. One clear emotion per face, because blended drawn faces are misread [S10].
6. **Show love with distance and orientation, not symbols:** proxemic zones, forward lean, facing toward, open arms [S11–S13]. Run a "distance script" alongside the colour script.
7. **Spot colour only where meaning is,** with intensity tracking emotion like a Pixar colour script [S14][S15]. Flat and minimal is not childish; symbols are [S16][S17].
8. **Humour = beat + reaction + say/show contrast** [S18][S19]. Put the joke in the picture, never in the fixed VO line.
9. **Key words: 1–3 words, on the spoken word, next to what they name.** Never a VO sentence on screen: redundancy hurt learning in 16 of 16 tests [S20].
10. **Pace by emotion.** Fast cutting helps calm content but overloads memory on arousing content [S24], and unrelated rapid cuts aren't remembered [S23]. Interrupt with related changes in kind, and slow down at peaks. For prompts, use narrative sentences, photographic framing words, positive phrasing for absence, and "change only X, keep everything else unchanged" edits [S25].

## 1. Shot size and emotion (close-ups, ECUs, reactions, inserts, shot-size rhythm)

**Principles**
1. Emotion outranks every other cutting criterion. Murch weights emotion 51%, story 23%, rhythm 10%, eye-trace 7%, 2D plane 5%, 3D space 4%, and says emotion "is worth more than all five of the things underneath it." [S1]
2. An extreme close-up works like "an exclamation point or BOLD text". Use it for emotion (eyes), for a detail the viewer must register, to say "this thing is important", or for a character's point of view. A push-in builds emphasis gradually; a cut to an ECU gives it instantly. [S2]
3. A change between two shots of the same subject has to be big. Cuts that are "neither subtle nor total" (e.g. full figure → a slightly tighter full figure) feel unmotivated and jumpy; the 20 mm / 30° rule says to change size or angle clearly. [S3]
4. Meaning comes from putting two shots side by side (Kuleshov). The same neutral face reads as hunger after soup and as grief after a coffin. A reaction shot shows the viewer how to feel about the shot before it. [S4]
5. Inserts of hands and objects show inner state without words ("a trembling hand or clenched fist can speak volumes"). Quick inserts also speed up a passage or break up a long talky one. [S5]
6. Contrast and affinity. The more contrast in a visual component (shot size, tone, colour, space), the higher the visual intensity; affinity (similarity) lowers it. Too little contrast loses the audience and constant contrast wears them out, so intensity has to be planned along the story. [S6]

**Apply to The Parent Code**
1. Write the beat's intended feeling first in each beat card ("viewer feels: dread / relief / recognition") and choose shot size, colour and background from that, as Thomas's point 14 asks.
2. Treat XCLOSE / eyes-only as bold type: give one to each emotional peak line, and add a slow Premiere push-in on the frame before it so the cut lands harder. *(No source gives a safe ECU ratio. Rationing is our inference: bold loses force if everything is bold.)*
3. Two frames in a row of the same character must jump at least two steps on the WIDE–MEDIUM–CLOSE–XCLOSE ladder, or change subject or angle. Make MEDIUM → MEDWIDE → MEDIUM runs (14 such stretches in V9) a QA failure.
4. Don't illustrate a statement; cut to the listener. Pair "trigger" frames (phone buzz, a door closing, "I'm fine") with a large-face reaction on white. The face doesn't need to be extreme, because the cut supplies context.
5. Give every chapter at least one HANDS insert that carries emotion: a hand hovering over the door handle, a thumb frozen above SEND, fingers squeezing the mug. These double as fast-cut beats in the dense first 30 s.
6. Plan intensity per chapter on a simple curve. Calm explanation uses affinity (white, similar sizes, slow pans). Peaks use maximum contrast (wide empty white → ECU, neutral → one bright colour). This is Thomas's point 10 in Block's terms.

## 2. Acting for animation: pose, gesture, expression (minimal characters)

**Principles**
1. "Thinking tends to lead to conclusions, and emotion tends to lead to action". "Acting is doing; acting is also reacting". "Your character should play an action until something happens to make him play a different action". People empathise with emotion, not information. [S7]
2. "A scene is negotiation": every two-person scene is a status transaction. Hooks also says the higher the character's power centre, the quicker its rhythm; the lower it is, the slower and heavier. [S7]
3. "Ma", the silence between beats, is extremely powerful when it is filled with emotion. Pauses are acting, not dead time. [S7]
4. Staging is "the presentation of any idea so that it is completely and unmistakably clear". One primary action has to dominate the silhouette, other body parts are "pulled along" by it, and a pose should read even as a solid black shape. [S8][S9]
5. Exaggeration means staying "true to reality, just presenting it in a wilder, more extreme form". Avoid "twins": mirrored left/right poses look "lifeless and dull". [S8]
6. Drawn faces are less reliable than we assume. In a test of McCloud's comic expressions, a majority only correctly named joy, cruelty, amazement and sadness. Fear was read as surprise, anger was mislabelled, and blended "complex" faces were unpredictable ("far more than the sum of their parts"). [S10]

**Apply to The Parent Code**
1. Give every frame's character a verb, never a state: "son pulls hood down over eyes", not "son is sad". Follow each trigger frame with a reaction frame, so the characters "clearly react" (Thomas's point 4).
2. Stage parent–teen frames as status negotiations, using height, who turns away and who takes up space. Use a low, sagging power centre for a defeated or slouching teen and a high, quick posture for an anxious, hovering parent.
3. Budget for "ma" frames: an awkward silence after an argument, or a parent stopping before reacting. Use one held frame (no pop-in, slow push) with both characters doing nothing visibly loaded, e.g. not looking at each other.
4. Thin-line bodies have almost no mass, so the silhouette is the whole performance. Prompt arms and hands away from the torso, in profile or ¾ view, with one dominant gesture per frame (a reaching hand, a raised palm, crossed arms clear of the body). This also keeps props apart from hands for mask reveals.
5. Write asymmetry into poses (weight on one leg, a head tilt, one shoulder raised, one hand in a pocket) and push each expression one notch past realistic, staying human.
6. For round stick-figure faces, use one clear emotion per frame at high intensity (eyebrows + mouth + eye direction) and let the body pose and the cut (Kuleshov, topic 1) carry nuance. Avoid prompting blends like "guilty but hopeful" into one face; split them across two frames instead. *(Splitting is our inference from [S10].)*

**Sources**
- [S7] Ed Hooks, *Acting for Animators* (Routledge, 4th ed.). His 7 essential acting concepts as listed by a student: http://adweaver.blogspot.com/2012/12/7-acting-principle-ed-hooks.html. His lecture notes on negotiation, power centre and "ma": https://adamthomson.blogspot.com/2010/11/ed-hooks-lecture-notes.html
- [S8] "Twelve basic principles of animation" (Wikipedia, after Thomas & Johnston, *The Illusion of Life*, 1981): https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twelve_basic_principles_of_animation
- [S9] John Kricfalusi, "Disney Principles: Staging 3 — Make your poses read well" (silhouette, line of action, primary action dominates): http://johnkstuff.blogspot.com/2009/01/disney-principles-staging-3-make-your.html

## 3. Love, trust and attention through behaviour (proxemics, orientation, immediacy)

**Principles**
1. Hall's interpersonal zones: intimate up to ~46 cm (touching, whispering); personal 0.46–1.22 m (good friends, family); social 1.2–3.7 m (acquaintances); public beyond 3.7 m. How far apart two people stand reads as how close they are. [S11]
2. Film maps shot size onto these zones: ECU/CU = intimate, MCU–MLS = personal, LS = social, ELS = public. The effect holds "regardless of the actual real camera distance", and the distance between characters on screen also signals their relationship. [S12]
3. Mehrabian's immediacy behaviours signal liking and approachability: "more forward lean, closer proximity, more eye gaze, more openness of arms and body, more direct body orientation". [S13]
4. Orientation shows intent: "we face, or turn, toward individuals that we want to interact with". Leaning back signals dislike or dismissal. Leaning forward + open = interest; forward + closed = possible aggression; back + open = cautious interest. [S13]
5. Contractive postures (making yourself small) create "an impression of anxiety, stress, or discomfort" and "less immediacy"; expansive postures read as confidence or power. [S13]

**Apply to The Parent Code**
1. Make the distance between MOM and SON a planned story variable, a "distance script" next to the colour script: social distance (doorway, opposite ends of the frame) for disconnection, personal (same bed edge, same sofa) for repair, intimate (hand on shoulder) only at a payoff.
2. Choose shot size by relationship, not location. Two small figures far apart on a white wide frame = emotional distance even in the same room. A tight two-shot where both heads nearly touch = closeness, with no symbol needed.
3. Replace every heart, star or speech-bubble symbol with an immediacy cue in the prompt, e.g. "Mom sits beside Son, leaning slightly toward him, shoulders turned to him, hands open on her knees, looking at him; Son looks at the floor".
4. Use mixed orientation for Thomas's best beats: "teen looking away but still listening" = torso and shoulders turned away, head turned a little back, eyes sliding toward the parent. "Pretends not to care" = closed arms + a sidelong glance.
5. Use lean + openness to separate parenting modes the script names: forward + closed (pointing, crossed arms, looming) = interrogation or "too many questions"; forward + open (palms up, at eye level) = support; back + closed = dismissal or "misreads silence as disrespect".
6. Draw the teen's shame or overwhelm as contraction (knees up, hood up, arms wrapped, small in frame). Draw relief as the same character opening up a few frames later, so the change is the emotional payoff. *(The pairing is our inference from [S13].)*
7. "Kneeling to the child's level" = equal head height in frame. Prompt eye-lines at the same height, natural eye-level camera (Thomas: exaggerated angles render badly).

**Sources**
- [S11] "Proxemics" (Wikipedia, after Edward T. Hall, *The Hidden Dimension*, 1966): https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proxemics
- [S12] Filmmakers Academy glossary, "Proxemic Patterns" (after Louis Giannetti, *Understanding Movies*): https://www.filmmakersacademy.com/glossary/proxemic-patterns/
- [S13] *Nonverbal Communication* (open textbook, Univ. of North Alabama), ch. 3 "Kinesics", citing Knapp et al. 2010 on Mehrabian's immediacy: https://una.pressbooks.pub/nonverbal-communication/chapter/chapter-3-kinesics/

## 4. Mature minimal style: white ground, line-only environments, one spot colour

**Principles**
1. Spot colour works because it is rare. The girl in the red coat is "one of the only color moments" in a black-and-white film, so the eye goes straight to her. It turns an abstract mass event into one personal moment, and it works as "a motif that operates on multiple levels", carrying meaning, not decoration. [S14]
2. Colour follows emotion over the whole film. Pixar's colour script was made early as small pastel sketches of every scene, and Lasseter: "the colors got more intense when the emotions were more intense, and when things got somber, it got very muted." [S15]
3. Adult-facing explainers on abstract emotional topics keep scenes extremely simple. A School of Life animator: "I tried to make the scenes as simple as possible… a single object lying in the centre of the composition", borrowing a live-action habit. [S16]
4. Flat, sparse, stylised design isn't inherently childish. UPA used flat colour, minimal suggested backgrounds and limited animation as an artistic choice for sophisticated, adult-facing films (*The Tell-Tale Heart*), against a field crowded with "funny animals". What reads childish is content and symbolism, not flatness. [S17]
5. Figure-ground needs tonal contrast. A dark or strong element on light ground gives the highest intensity; similar tones side by side flatten the frame (Block's tonal contrast, see [S6]). This is why white characters vanish on white without a soft tone behind them.
6. *(No source found for a rule on "what makes an illustration look childish". The traits Thomas named (full-frame single-hue rooms, generic symbols like hearts and stars, every object coloured) are his taste, not a researched standard.)*

**Apply to The Parent Code**
1. One spot colour per frame, on the emotional object or the emotional moment only. Everything else is thin black line on white (door outline, bed outline, one chair). Assign each recurring object a fixed colour (e.g. the phone is always the same turquoise) so that when it returns it is recognised and can mean something new (Thomas's point 11).
2. Build the colour script before prompting. White or neutral is the calm default. Muted grey/navy is for heavy or night beats. A full-colour emotional field appears only at the 3–5 biggest peaks, and colour intensity rises with emotional intensity (Lasseter). This turns the 70–80% neutral / 20–30% accent target into a per-chapter plan.
3. For abstract ideas, the default frame is one object or one face centred on white with large negative space, the School of Life move. Use it for "key idea" lines before reaching for a metaphor scene.
4. Get maturity from behaviour and restraint, not from redesigning the characters. Keep the stick figures, delete the symbol props (heart phone, gold star, trophy, smile mask from V9's list) and put human situations in their place, as UPA did.
5. Keep the soft tone patch (very light grey/beige/blue) behind white characters on white grounds. Treat it as figure-ground, not decoration.

**Sources**

- [S15] MoMA audio guide, "Colorscript (detail), Toy Story, 1995" (John Lasseter on Ralph Eggleston's colour script): https://www.moma.org/audio/playlist/192/2581

## 5. Subtle humour: timing, understatement, say/show contrast, reactions

**Principles**
1. Comic timing is "the use of pacing and delivery to enhance the effectiveness of comedy". Writers and performers "insert a dramatic pause or beat to allow the audience to discern the subtext" before the punchline. [S18]
2. Juxtaposition makes the audience compare two things "simply because they are placed next to each other". An unexpected comparison creates irony. Understatement creates irony when a character's response "does not match how the viewer expects". [S18]
3. Comedy also comes from "quick juxtaposition between fast and slow timing". [S18]
4. Visual comedy can come from the frame itself: objects entering frame in funny ways, people exiting frame, "there and back again" (a character moves on, notices, comes back, and the reaction reveals character), matched transitions, and perfectly timed sound effects. "The frame is a playground, so play." [S19]
5. The reaction shot carries the joke as much as the gag does: the cut to the face tells the audience what the moment means (Kuleshov, see [S4]).

**Apply to The Parent Code**
1. Build every humour beat as setup frame → held beat → reaction close-up (e.g. the "seriously?" look). The hold is a still frame with no pop-in and no camera move. *(No source gives a hold length; set it by ear in the edit.)*
2. Put humour in the picture, not the line. The VO stays fixed while the frame contradicts it: VO "most parents stay calm" over Mom's twitching eye at the dishwasher; "I'm fine" over a hood-up son and an untouched bowl. This is how to add jokes without touching Muhammad's script.
3. Prefer understatement for the adult tone: a tiny reaction to a big moment (one raised eyebrow, a flat mouth, a slow blink). When Thomas asks for an "exaggerated parent reaction", push it through a huge face on white, not cartoon effects (sweat drops, swirling eyes). *(The ban on cartoon effects is our inference, in line with "not childish".)*
4. Thomas's "parent walks past the room, notices, comes back" is Wright's "there and back again". Do it in three frames: Mom walking past the doorway (wide) → Mom's eyes sliding back (ECU) → Mom leaning back into the doorway (medium). The reaction is the punchline.
5. Use exits and entrances with stills. The empty frame is a punchline (cut to the empty chair or the shut door where the teen just was), and a pop-in prop can "enter" on a VO word with a timed sound effect.
6. Contrast fast and slow: stack quick pop-ins of a parent's questions ("Who? Where? Why? Did you eat?"), then cut to a dead-still close-up of the teen's flat face, then return to the serious point.

**Sources**
- [S18] "Comedic device" (Wikipedia: comic timing and the beat, juxtaposition, understatement): https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comedic_device
- [S19] MovieMaker, summary of Tony Zhou / Every Frame a Painting, "Edgar Wright — How to Do Visual Comedy" (2014): https://www.moviemaker.com/edgar-wright-visual-comedy-video/

## 6. On-screen key words (how many, when, how long, typography)

**Principles**
1. **Redundancy principle**: people learn more deeply from graphics + narration than from graphics + narration + on-screen text. Supported in 16 of 16 experimental tests, median effect size d = 0.86. Full captions of the VO over the picture hurt. [S20]
2. **Signaling principle**: cues that "highlight the organization of the essential material" help. Supported in 24 of 28 tests, median d = 0.41. [S20]
3. **The key-word exception**: adding a single key word or short phrase printed next to the matching animation graphic improved retention (d = 0.47–0.70) but not transfer (d = 0.04–0.15) (Mayer & Johnson 2008). Short labels help memory; sentences don't. [S20]
4. **Coherence principle**: extraneous "interesting" details can lower deeper learning (with a boundary: Park et al. 2011 found such details helped under low cognitive load). Text that decorates instead of naming the idea is a cost. [S20]
5. **Reading time**: BBC subtitle guidance (via a summary) recommends 160–180 wpm, about 0.33–0.375 s per word, and a minimum of about 0.3 s per word on screen. That floor is for subtitles on TV, not designed key words. [S21]
6. *(No credible source found that sets a number of key words per minute, or typography rules for kinetic emphasis words. Anything below on budget and type is our proposal, not research.)*

**Apply to The Parent Code**
1. Never put a VO sentence on screen. A text moment is one to three words: TRUST, SAFE, LISTEN, NOT REJECTION.
2. Make the word the VO's own key word. It pops in on that spoken word (temporal match) and sits next to the face or object it names, in the frame's negative space (spatial match), as in Mayer & Johnson's label condition.
3. Use words as signals of structure and stakes: chapter numbers count as signals, but idea words should mark the one concept a chapter turns on. V9 had 7 chapter numbers and only 4 idea words. *(Proposal: about one idea word per chapter plus the hook and the ending, which keeps Thomas's "not constantly".)*
4. Let a text frame double as a pattern interrupt: a white frame with one word in black, or in the chapter's spot colour, after a run of character frames.
5. Hold each word at least to the end of the VO phrase it belongs to. The ~0.3 s/word subtitle floor is only a minimum; a designed impact word needs longer to land. *(The hold length is our inference.)*
6. Typography proposal *(unsourced)*: one bold sans in caps, the same everywhere, black or the spot colour only, no outlines, bounces or emoji. A plain cut or soft scale-in reads as mature; bouncy kinetic type reads as kids' content (Thomas's point 2).

**Sources**
- [S20] Richard E. Mayer & Logan Fiorella, "Principles for reducing extraneous processing in multimedia learning: coherence, signaling, redundancy, spatial contiguity, and temporal contiguity principles", *The Cambridge Handbook of Multimedia Learning*, ch. 12: https://edtechuvic.ca/wp-content/uploads/sites/11/2022/09/principles-for-reducing-extraneous-processing-in-multimedia-learning-coherence-signaling-redundancy-spatial-contiguity-and-temporal-contiguity-principles.pdf

## 7. Retention and pattern interrupts (what helps, what over-cutting costs)

**Principles**
1. YouTube's own retention model: **Intro** = % of viewers still watching after the first 30 s. **Top moments** = almost no one dropped off. **Spikes** = rewatched or shared (interest, or something unclear). **Dips** = skipped or abandoned. YouTube's advice: "Modify the first 30 seconds of your video and experiment with different styles", and move compelling content earlier. [S22]
2. Cuts and other structural features trigger automatic orienting responses, a reflexive spike of attention to novelty or change (Limited Capacity Model, LC4MP). [S23]
3. Related edits (same content, new angle) can reinforce memory, but "unrelated cuts do not make it into memory, especially when the cuts are coming at the message processor rapidly" (Lang et al. 2000). With complex messages, adding attention-grabbing features "tends to lead to decrements in memory". [S23]
4. Fast pace × arousing content = overload. For calm messages, recognition improved as pacing rose from slow to medium to fast. For arousing messages, recognition peaked at medium pace and dropped significantly at fast pace: "the combination of fast pace and arousing content overloads the processing system" (Lang, Bolls, Potter & Kawahara 1999). [S24]
5. *(Evidence gap: "pattern interrupt" is creator jargon with no research definition. No credible study found gives an optimal interval between visual changes on YouTube. Circulating "change every X seconds" rules are unsourced, including an auto-generated "3–5 edits/minute" claim on an academia.edu summary, which was rejected.)* The research equivalents are orienting responses to change [S23] and Block's contrast/affinity [S6].

**Apply to The Parent Code**
1. Pace by emotion, which is the key finding: in calm explanation, cut faster and add pop-ins and interrupts. At arousing peaks (shame, conflict, the big realisation), slow down to fewer, held, related frames, e.g. the 3–5 sequential stills of one moment Thomas approved. This reconciles "more pattern interrupts" with his "still moment vs movement" contrast.
2. Make interrupts related: an ECU of the character who is talking, the same object at a new scale, the VO's key word, a metaphor tied to the line. A random unrelated image grabs the eye but, per [S23], doesn't get remembered.
3. ~300 stills in ~8.5 min already means a new image roughly every 1.7 s on average, so more cuts are not the lever. What counts is a change in kind: a two-step size jump, a switch to a white ground, a colour field, a text frame, a held silence. *(The 1.7 s is arithmetic from the brief, not a source.)*
4. Proposed QA definition of an interrupt: a frame that differs from the one before on at least two axes (shot size step ≥ 2, background type, colour field, text, subject). Check that one lands about every 20–30 s, which is Thomas's own target and does not come from research.
5. Front-load contrast in the first 30 s (YouTube's "Intro" metric) and test variants across videos, as YouTube advises.
6. After each upload, read the retention graph in YouTube Studio against the frame list: mark dips and check whether they fall in medium/wide room stretches or text-heavy beats. Treat spikes as possible confusion as well as interest. This turns Thomas's taste into data for the next video.

## 8. AI image prompting for expressions, close-ups, white line-art grounds, one accent, consistency

**Principles (Google's own guidance for Gemini image / "Nano Banana")**
1. "Describe the scene, don't just list keywords." A narrative, descriptive paragraph beats a list of disconnected words. [S25]
2. Be hyper-specific: replace a generic term with a concrete description (not "fantasy armor" but "ornate elven plate armor, etched with silver leaf patterns…"). [S25]
3. Provide context and intent: say what the image is for, which steers style and tone. [S25]
4. Use semantic negative prompts: describe the wanted absence positively ("an empty, deserted street with no signs of traffic") instead of "no cars". [S25]
5. Control the camera with photographic language: "wide-angle shot", "macro shot", "85mm portrait lens", "Dutch angle". [S25]
6. For stylised illustration, state the style, key characteristics, colour palette, line style and shading, and write "The background must be white" when needed. Minimalist design = a single subject placed deliberately on a vast empty canvas. [S25]
7. Edit, don't regenerate: "Using the provided image, change only the [element] to [description]. Keep everything else unchanged." Conversational follow-ups also work: "Keep everything the same, but change the character's expression to be more serious." [S25]
8. Stated limitation: "absolute character consistency across multiple images" can need follow-up refinement. [S25]
9. *(Evidence gap: no Google source says long prompts fail. Google recommends detailed narrative prompts. "Long prompts break the model" is unverified community lore; our inference is that the real risk is conflicting or stacked instructions, not length.)*

**Apply to The Parent Code**
1. Write each prompt as one paragraph of sentences in a fixed order: purpose → camera/shot → who + action + face mechanics → environment as line-only → colour rule → background rule. No keyword lists.
2. Describe faces as mechanics, not labels (hyper-specific [S25] + drawn faces are misread [S10]): "eyebrows pushed hard together and up in the middle, eyes wide and glossy, mouth a small tight down-turned line, chin tucked, shoulders raised to the ears", not "worried". One emotion per frame, body included.
3. Name the framing like a photographer, at a natural eye-level angle: "extreme close-up: Son's face fills the entire frame, cropped at the forehead and chin"; "macro shot of Mom's hand hovering just above the door handle"; "wide shot: two small figures far apart with large empty white space between them". Avoid Dutch or extreme angles (Thomas: they render badly).
4. Phrase the white ground and the single accent positively, per [S25]: "The background must be white. The room is suggested only by a thin black outline of a door, drawn in the same thin line as the characters, with no fill. The only coloured element in the whole image is the turquoise phone in his hand; everything else is black line on white." Avoid "no furniture, no colour" lists.
5. Open every prompt with a context line, e.g. "A frame from a mature, understated parenting explainer for parents and teenagers." *(That this specifically reduces childish output is our inference from the "context and intent" principle; test it A/B.)*
6. Use Google's inpainting template verbatim for every in-scene change (the ≥30% edit-prompt frames): "Using the provided image, change only Son's face to [mechanics]. Keep everything else unchanged." This is also the most reliable way to make 3–5 sequential stills of one moment stay consistent.
7. For consistency, refer to the references the same way every time ("MOM from reference image 1, SON from reference image 2"). Build sequences by editing the last approved frame, not from fresh prompts, and budget re-rolls for drift (Google admits consistency can need refinement). *(Proposal, untested: if Flow accepts an extra image as a style ingredient, add one approved white-ground frame to lock the minimal look.)*
