## A. Thomas's feedback on "Seven Things Your Child Can't Tell You" — long version (verbatim)

1. Background colors need to be reduced much more
• Too many scenes are still completely built around one color tone, especially blue, beige, or green.
• When the wall, floor, furniture, and surrounding objects all have similar colors, the frame becomes flat and repetitive.
• This can make the video feel more like a basic explainer or children's animation.
• I would strongly prefer: white or very light neutral backgrounds as the default; only the important object or emotional element should carry strong color; background environments can often be shown only with simple outlines; do not fully color every object just because it exists in the scene.
Example: If the phone is important, let the phone stand out. If the face is important, keep almost everything else neutral.
2. The visuals need to feel more mature
• We are not creating a kids channel. • The audience is parents and teenagers. • Some symbols still feel too simple or childish. • For example, the smartphone with the large heart feels too basic and too much like children's content.
Instead of simple symbols, show emotions through: eye contact, hesitation, body language, distance between people, hands, reactions, silence, facial expressions, real interactions.
3. Love and connection should be shown through behavior
If we want to show love, trust, attention, or emotional safety, I would prefer situations like: a parent quietly sitting next to a teenager; a teen looking away but still listening; a hand reaching out; a parent stopping before reacting; a child hesitating before telling the truth; someone noticing the other person is uncomfortable; a parent kneeling down to the child's level; two people sitting together without needing a heart symbol. This feels much more real and emotionally powerful.
4. We need stronger facial expressions
This is one of the biggest areas we can still improve. Please use more: eyes, eyebrows, mouth movement, head position, hand gestures, posture, eye direction. The characters should not just "stand in the scene." They should clearly react.
5. Bring the camera closer
We need more: close-ups, extreme close-ups, face-only moments, face + hands, emotional details. Sometimes a large face on a white background is much stronger than a complete room. Important emotional lines should visually feel important.
6. More variation in camera distance
Please avoid staying too long in the same medium/wide framing. Use more variation: wide shot, medium shot, close-up, extreme close-up, object close-up, reaction shot. The viewer should feel visual changes throughout the video.
7. More real everyday parenting situations
We need more moments that parents and teenagers immediately recognize from real life. Examples: teen says "I'm fine" but clearly is not; parent asks too many questions; teenager rolls their eyes; child hides something; parent walks past the room, notices something is wrong, and comes back; teen wants attention but pretends not to care; parent misunderstands silence as disrespect; child starts to say something but changes their mind; parent tries to help but accidentally makes the situation worse; awkward silence after an argument. These scenes will make the psychology feel much more real.
8. More subtle humor
We also need more humor, but not childish humor. Use small relatable moments like: exaggerated parent reaction; teenager giving a "seriously?" look; awkward pause; parent overthinking something simple; visual contradiction between what someone says and what they actually do; short funny reaction before returning to the serious topic. This gives the video more personality and keeps it entertaining.
9. More strong on-screen words
We are still missing some short text moments. Not too much. But selected key words can strengthen important ideas. Examples: TRUST, SAFE, LISTEN, TESTING, SHAME, CONTROL, MISREAD, ATTENTION, OVERWHELMED, HONESTY, NOT REJECTION. These should appear only at strong moments, not constantly.
10. Use stronger contrast inside each frame
Contrast should not only mean black versus white. We also need contrast between: empty background vs strong object; neutral scene vs one bright color; wide shot vs extreme close-up; serious moment vs humorous reaction; still moment vs movement; small prop vs large face; silence vs strong visual beat. This is what keeps attention.
11. Recurring visual ideas are good
The jar concept is a good example. I like that some visual elements return later in the video. Please continue doing this. But every recurring object should: have a reason; evolve with the story; mean something different later; support the emotional progression. It should not return only because it looks good.
12. Fewer objects, stronger meaning
This is very important. I would rather have: one strong object, one strong face, one strong gesture than: bed, chair, lamp, table, shelf, wall, decoration, several small props all at the same time. The viewer should always know immediately where to look.
13. Reduce full room scenes
We do not need to fully illustrate every location. Sometimes just: one door outline, one bed outline, one chair, one object is enough to communicate the place. The story should stay focused on the characters.
14. Emotion must come before decoration
For every scene, ask: What is the viewer supposed to feel here? Only after that should we decide: background, prop, color, camera angle, movement. Emotion should lead the visual decision.
15. More pattern interrupts
The movement inside frames is better, but movement alone is not enough. We also need stronger visual switches: suddenly white background; extreme close-up; one-word text; unexpected object; funny reaction; strong pose; short metaphor; sudden silence visually; large facial reaction. These moments help retention.
16. The main principle — "Less, but stronger."
Less: background, decoration, full-screen color, random props, generic symbols.
More: emotion, facial expressions, gestures, relatable situations, contrast, close-ups, meaningful color, strong visual ideas, humor, memorable moments.
17. (open question about real image material — later answered, see D)
Overall: make the videos feel more mature, more emotional, more focused, more relatable, more visually varied, less like a standard animated explainer.

## C. Muhammad's reply that Thomas APPROVED ("Thanks Ukasha, exactly. Your summary captures the direction very well.")
Backgrounds: White or very light neutral will be the default. Places are shown with a few simple outlines (a door, a bed, a chair), not full rooms. Strong colour goes only on the important object or the emotional moment. Full coloured scenes stay for emotional peaks and night scenes.
More mature visuals: No more simple symbols like the heart phone. Love, trust and attention will come from behaviour: a parent sitting next to the teen, a hand reaching out, eye contact, hesitation, distance between people, a parent pausing before reacting.
Emotion and camera: Stronger faces, eyes, hands and body language. Many more close-ups and face-only moments on white. The camera distance changes more often, so we never stay long in medium or wide shots.
Real life and humour: More everyday moments parents and teens recognise: "I'm fine" when he's not, too many questions, eye rolls, awkward silence. Small, smart humour like a "seriously?" look or a parent overthinking something.
Key words: A few strong on-screen words at the important moments (TRUST, SAFE, LISTEN…), not constantly.
Recurring objects: These only come back when they mean something new. Fewer elements per frame, so it's always clear where to look.

I think it makes more sense to keep refining and strengthening our own visual language."

"It shows how important it is that we define the emotional beats, camera angles, reactions, and visual contrasts very clearly before production starts. I do not simply want 'more images.' I want better and more varied images with a clear purpose. "If we keep improving the style step by step and stay consistent, we can create something that feels truly unique, recognizable…"
