# Idea, not object — "don't illustrate the sentence"

Before writing or reviewing any frame, ask:

> **"What should the viewer FEEL and understand after seeing this frame?"**
> not
> **"What object or word does this sentence contain?"**

This is the most-repeated note from both clients this workflow has served. History: in Video 2 many
frames answered their own line by putting its noun in the character's hands ("They enjoy making videos"
→ a boy holding a camera). Thomas rated the video 8.5/10 with consistency fixed and said this thinking,
applied from the first version, is what takes it to 10.

> **v19:** `v19-principles.md` §0.4 and §4 win over anything here. For every line, explore every direction — the real
> everyday moment, the behaviour, a reaction, an object, a contrast, humour, a metaphor, an unexpected angle — and keep
> going until one idea is clearly the strongest; record the runner-ups (`idea`, `alt` in `shot-plan-v19.md`). Real
> everyday moments carry the everyday lines (`everyday-situations-v19.md`); love, trust, attention and emotional safety
> are shown through behaviour, never a symbol (`acting-and-interaction-v19.md`); a metaphor is used where it adds meaning
> the words don't already carry (`props-symbols-metaphors-v19.md`).

## Thomas's four questions — ask them on every important line
1. What is the **emotion** behind this sentence?
2. What should the viewer **understand or feel** here?
3. Can I show a **reaction, consequence, contrast or progression** instead of the object being named?
4. Would a **close-up, a character interaction, a visual metaphor or a small story** make the point
   stronger?

And his framing of the rule: *"The rule is not: every scene needs a metaphor. The rule is: every
important scene should communicate something, not just decorate the narration."* Simple visuals are
fine on ordinary beats. On emotional and psychological beats, push the storytelling, expressions,
reactions and visual logic much further. When in doubt: *"What is the most interesting and unexpected
way to visually tell this sentence?"*

## The failure pattern

| Narration | Wrong instinct (draws the noun) | What the line is about | Right instinct (draws the idea) |
|---|---|---|---|
| "Your teen has so many interests." | a boy at a desk with five hobby objects | being pulled in every direction | the boy small in the centre while a huge guitar, camera, laptop and books tower over him (his own example) |
| "He did not stop caring. He stopped trying to be heard." | a sad boy on his bed | the process of giving up | tries to speak → talked over → tries again → hand drops → eyes leave her (his own example, as a sequence) |
| "It wasn't obedience. It was influence." | a hug | voluntary choice vs control | the mother speaks calmly, he looks away, then turns back on his own (an on-screen idea word can land in Premiere; never drawn into the image) |
| "Teens need to feel in control." | a teen holding a steering wheel icon | autonomy where it's safe | he's actually driving on a clearly safe empty road, her hands relaxed in her lap |
| "Small moments add up." | a clock | deposits into a relationship | a coin dropping into a jar that fills a little more each time |
| "Pressure builds." | a stressed face | invisible build-up | the kettle on the hob coming to the boil while the family eats in silence |
| "Not every rule has to go." | a list of rules | unnecessary control vs real safety | she lifts away grey walls while her foot holds the one blue boundary wall in place |

The wrong instinct finds a literal noun or a generic mood and stops. The right one asks what the
sentence argues or reveals and stages *that* — usually a contrast, a before/after, a reaction, a
consequence or a progression.

## Checklist per line
1. Does the line hold a claim, contrast, contradiction, realisation or comparison, even casually
   phrased? Then that structure, not the nearest noun, is what the frame shows.
2. Hide the narration: would a viewer land on the point, or only on "there's a [noun] in it"?
3. Reaching for a generic mood pose (tired, sad on a sofa) because the line felt abstract? Stop. Find
   the concrete before/after, reaction or consequence.
4. Comparison or contradiction lines: show both halves in one frame (a split, two states, before and
   after) rather than one half and hoping the narration carries the other.
5. Is the idea precise? Thomas caught a hug standing in for "influence" — it said love, not influence.
   Make sure the image says exactly what the line says, not a nearby warmer idea.

## Metaphors — when a line earns one
- Build the metaphors the script literally asks for ("think of it like…"), and reach for one on a
  psychological line where it adds meaning the words don't already carry — pressure, overwhelm, talent,
  confusion, freedom, a decision, inner conflict. Not every line needs one. Thomas's vocabulary (Video 3):
  **an obstacle in the path, a crossroads, a race, a balancing act, a maze, falling dominoes**; and on
  5 Oct: "stronger visual metaphors", "better and more mature props", "not… a kids channel". Seed
  examples proven on this channel: tug-of-war (the rope goes
  slack), puzzle pieces fitting, sand escaping a squeezing fist, a coin jar filling, a boiler under
  pressure, a tennis net (opponent vs coach), a dashboard warning light (signal vs the real problem),
  walls (some removable, one stays), a tool board where every tool hangs in its painted outline.
- The meaning lives in the object's **physics**, needing no label. Pick objects everyone already knows.
- One metaphor per idea, one frame or a short run of frames, then it's over. Never stack three
  metaphors into one abstract space (the cut Video 2 maze), never build a recurring invented system
  (the rejected cord, photographs, drawings).
- A character is in the metaphor and reacts to it whenever possible — CHARACTER → EMOTION → ACTION →
  SYMBOL. A metaphor with nobody in it is a diagram.
- When a segment dramatises an abstract idea, pick a **place whose physics everyone knows** and find
  the one object in it that *is* the idea (Video 2's repair garage tool board carried a whole
  segment on "entrenchment").
- No share of frames: the moment decides. Too many metaphors in a row read as diagrams; a metaphor that
  only repeats the line is decoration. Never hearts, stars, trophies, emoji-style icons or picture
  bubbles standing in for a feeling.

## The check
Frames where a single character just holds the object named in their own line are listed by the checks
(from each PROP's `nouns` list) for a second look: is there a stronger idea — a reaction, a consequence,
a contrast, a real moment? The number is information, never a target.

## Scope
Apply the principle to every script. Don't copy a specific example (a coin jar, a garage) into an
unrelated video unless the new script genuinely calls for it.
