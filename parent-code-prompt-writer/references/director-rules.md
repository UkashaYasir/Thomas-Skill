# Director Rules — The Parent Code

The story-craft, camera and construction rules for The Parent Code — what makes the frames read
as a film. Built around Thomas's emotion-first rule, his priority order, his tests, and his taste for
scale, purposeful colour and surprise.

> **v19:** `v19-principles.md` wins over anything here, then `RULES-CARD.md` and the top of SKILL.md. Lines below that
> disagreed with v19 have been rewritten and point to the v19 file that now holds the detail. Thomas's 5 Oct principle:
> "Less, but stronger." — less background, decoration, full-screen colour, random props and generic symbols; more
> emotion, faces, gestures, relatable situations, contrast, close-ups, meaningful colour, strong ideas, humour.

---

## PART 1 — FOUNDATION

### 1. The philosophy
This system does not exist to make beautiful images. It exists to make videos people keep watching
for eight and a half minutes. Every image helps tell the story; the viewer never feels they are
watching a slideshow of unrelated illustrations. Think like a film director, not an illustrator:
an illustrator makes a nice picture, a director makes the next moment.

### 2. The golden rule
Every image answers one question: what is the single most important thing the viewer should feel
and understand in these few seconds? One thing. A frame trying to say several unrelated things fails.

### 2.5 Priority system
When rules seem to conflict, follow this order:
1. **Emotion and story clarity** — the viewer feels and understands the moment
2. **Viewer understanding without sound** — the sound-off and pause tests (§11)
3. **Story continuity** — time, state and consequences carry forward
4. **Character and object consistency**
5. **Composition, camera and colour direction**
6. **Beauty**

And Thomas's own order for what carries meaning, never inverted:
**CHARACTER → EMOTION → ACTION → SYMBOL**. Never SYMBOL → TEXT → CHARACTER. A strong object or an idea
word may reinforce what a character is living; it never replaces it. Since 5 Oct generic symbols (icons,
ticks, crosses, hearts, stars) are on Thomas's "Less" list — emotion is shown through "eye contact,
hesitation, body language, distance between people, hands, reactions, silence, facial expressions, real
interactions."

### 3. Story first
The script always comes first. Never design an image because it looks cool or is technically
impressive. Every visual earns its place by making the moment faster to feel and understand.

### 4. Film, not slideshow
The viewer should feel like watching an animated documentary, never a presentation. Pressing Play,
not clicking Next.

### 5. Continuous storytelling
Characters don't teleport, objects don't randomly appear, time doesn't jump back. Prefer
Scene A → A changes → A develops → A ends over A → B → C. Small changes create movement; movement
creates immersion. What holds a Parent Code film together is **time that only moves forward, a main
character whose state changes step by step and never resets, and consequences you can point at**
(she reads the letter in segment 3; in segment 7 it's still on the fridge, crumpled). Not an
invented recurring prop — Thomas rejected three attempts at one.

### 6. Every image moves the story forward
A frame introduces information, reveals something, continues an action, shows a reaction, raises
tension, or simplifies an idea. If it does none of these, delete it.

### 7. The viewer never waits
No filler images, no decorative shots, no random landscapes, no meaningless poses, and no artificially
long held shots to pad runtime (Thomas standard 9).

### 8. Fewer things, stronger things
Complexity kills clarity. If removing an object makes the idea clearer, remove it. This is about the
**number of elements**, never about energy: Thomas wants fewer things in a frame *and* more drama —
the resolution is that intensity comes from scale, camera, expression and one strong colour, never
from cramming. A dramatic frame usually has fewer things in it than a calm one, all pointing at the
one that matters. Thomas, 5 Oct: *"one strong object, one strong face, one strong gesture"* rather than
*"bed, chair, lamp, table, shelf, wall, decoration, several small props all at the same time."* And the things that
remain are finished and specific — the hero carries the few details that make it that object; minimal means few
objects, never half-drawn ones. A place shown as an outline is still finished: a complete, closed line drawing.

### 9. Beer-mat rule
Explaining it on a beer mat, you wouldn't draw ten objects. You'd draw one clear symbol or one clear
moment. Every frame should feel like that.

### 10. The glance test
Look at it briefly, then hide it. Could someone say what it was about — and what it felt like?
If not, it's too complicated.

### 11. Thomas's two tests
- **Sound-off test** — if the viewer mutes the video, can they roughly understand what is happening?
- **Pause test** — if the viewer pauses on one single frame, can they read the emotion?
The narration explains details; it never rescues a confusing or flat visual.

### 12. Every image needs a purpose
Thomas standard 10: every scene has a clear purpose — **emotion, information, or holding attention**.
A frame doing none of the three is cut.

### 13. Don't draw sentences
Illustrate meaning, never grammar. One sentence may need several images; several sentences may need one.
Divide by visual ideas, never by punctuation (the line-per-prompt format still holds — this is about
what each line's frame shows).

### 14. Visual beats
Think in beats: opens the letter → reads → realises → shocked → drops it. Each beat its own image,
not one image trying to show everything.

### 15. Never repeat information — and never recycle a shot
If frame 12 established the character is alone, frame 13 doesn't simply show the same lonely
character again. Something changes: expression, camera, object, movement, focus, colour, story.
When a place and pose genuinely recur, change the camera distance, the hand action, the expression or
the whole composition — Thomas spots recycled shots (the mother with the cup at 4:17 and 4:27).

### 16. "Why am I seeing this?"
If the answer is "because the narration says so," rewrite it. If it's "because this makes the
viewer feel or understand," keep it.

### 17. Emotion before information — the main rule of this channel
Thomas: *"Before designing a scene, ask: what should the viewer FEEL here? Then build the facial
expression, eyes, body language, hands, distance, camera framing, object and colour around that
emotion. The characters must ACT the narration."* Humans notice emotion first, facts second. Show
surprise, hurt, relief, tension before numbers or explanations. Emotion always comes from the story.
Full build order and vocabulary: `emotion-and-performance.md`.

### 18. Curiosity is visual
Curiosity comes from incomplete information: show the one mysterious part before the whole machine,
what the character notices before the whole room. The viewer should always want the next frame.
This is doubly true in the opening.

### 19. Visual rhythm
Bad rhythm: character, character, character, character, all at the same medium distance. Good rhythm
alternates — Thomas's own list, 5 Oct — **"wide shot, medium shot, close-up, extreme close-up, object
close-up, reaction shot"**, plus face-only moments, face + hands and hands-only shots. "Avoid staying too
long in the same medium/wide framing." The same character in back-to-back frames changes size or angle
clearly. Rhythm also comes from contrast and pattern interrupts — a sudden white background, an extreme
close-up, a one-word text, an unexpected object, a funny reaction, a strong pose, a short metaphor, a
visual silence, a large facial reaction (`humour-text-contrast-v19.md`) — not from changing background
colours. Scene variety comes from storytelling, never from random changes.

### 20. Every frame feels intentional
Every pose, object, colour, camera angle, expression and empty area exists because it improves the
story — never because it was easy to generate.

## PART 2 — THE DIRECTOR SYSTEM

### 21. Think like a film director
Never "what picture should I draw?" Always "what would the audience see next if this were a movie?"

### 22. The storyboard rule
Every script becomes a storyboard before it becomes prompts. Many sentences describe an invisible
idea; storyboard the thought, not the wording. Wrong: person, person, person. Right: hand opening the
envelope → eyes on the first line → close-up of the surprised face → the letter slipping → everything
feels different. **Cause and effect is shown, not summarised** — Thomas's withdrawal example: the teen
tries to explain → the parent talks over him → he tries again → she stays controlling → his
expression changes → his hand drops → he stops talking → his eyes move away → his shoulders fall.

### 23. Visual decision hierarchy
Before each frame decide, in order: what should the viewer **feel**? what **changed**? what does the
viewer need to **notice**? **who** notices it? what is the **simplest strong** way to show it?
Never start with "what should the character do?"

### 23.5 Visualise the insight, not the sentence
Every line holds one core insight; the frame communicates that insight, through actions, contrasts,
consequences or reactions — especially when the line describes a realisation, comparison,
contradiction, psychological change or invisible process. See `idea-not-object.md`.

### 24. Every frame has one hero
Exactly one visual hero: a character's face, a pair of hands, an object, a place.
Everything else supports it; nothing competes. When the hero is an object, it carries the frame's one
colour element (`style-and-colour-v19.md`), so composition and colour point the eye at the same place;
when the hero is a face, nothing else carries strong colour. By default a character is the hero when the
frame is about their experience; choose an object only when it genuinely is the subject.

### 25. The focus rule
At a glance the viewer knows what the image is about — "The viewer should always know immediately
where to look." If the eye wanders searching, the frame is too busy. Simplify.

### 26. Change something
Between consecutive frames something major changes: position, expression, camera distance,
angle, object state, composition, scale, focus, story. Across the whole film no composition comes back
unchanged — Thomas: "the viewer should still never feel like the visuals are repeating or that the same
composition keeps coming back."

### 27. Camera is storytelling
Every prompt states shot size and angle explicitly — an unstated camera is a random camera. Thomas,
5 Oct: "Bring the camera closer." Detail: `camera-and-closeups-v19.md`.
- **Extreme close-up** — a detail, a discovery, the eyes, a trembling hand.
- **Large face / face-only** — the emotional lines; the head fills most of the frame (Thomas: "Faces can
  occupy 60–80%"), often alone on pure white — "Sometimes a large face on a white background is much
  stronger than a complete room." The head shape stays whole, with open space on the side the eyes look.
- **Face + hands** — the face and the hands that carry the feeling in one frame.
- **Reaction shot** — the listener's face answering what was just said or done.
- **Close-up** — expressions, important objects, reading, thinking.
- **Hands-only** — cropped at the forearms, head out of frame: grabbing, letting go, a hand dropping.
- **Medium** — dialogue, interaction, movement.
- **Wide** — relationships, distance between people, isolation, scale.
- **Over-the-shoulder** — arguments; the viewer shares one side.
- **Profile two-shot** — connection, two people side by side.
- **Low angle** — someone holding their ground, power, an object looming.
- **High angle** — someone beaten down, withdrawing, feeling small.
- **Overhead** — systems, a table seen from above, a person lying awake.
- **Square-on** — metaphor objects, split-screen comparisons.
Thomas: *"Wide shots are good for relationships and body position. Close-ups are needed for emotion.
We need both."* Natural camera positions; low and high angles only with a reason (exaggerated angles
render badly).

### 28. Vary the composition — go big
The same layout over and over gets repetitive: between scenes, place the subject left, right, higher,
lower, and change who is near and far; inside one scene characters keep their screen sides (180° rule).
Large faces are a strength of this style: give every important emotional line one, with open space on
the side the eyes look and the head shape whole — never a random crop. Spread them through the whole
film wherever the emotion is, not bunched and not rationed.

### 29. Use the space
Space around the hero directs attention and makes the hero feel larger. In this channel that space is
white or very light neutral by default — "Sometimes a large face on a white background is much stronger
than a complete room." The energy comes from the face, the camera distance and the one colour element,
not from filling the space with colour. A full-colour field is kept for planned emotional peaks and night,
and never sits behind a face close-up (`style-and-colour-v19.md`).

### 30. Visual hierarchy
The eye meets things in this order: 1 main idea (the hero), 2 supporting object or second character,
3 background, 4 details. If details compete with the main idea, remove them. Colour follows the same
order: only the important object or emotional element carries strong colour; the background is white or
very light neutral with thin soft-grey outlines.

### 31. Body language and face together
People read posture faster than faces, and Thomas also wants faces that read instantly. Build both:
body first, then pose, then hands, then eyes, eyebrows and mouth. If the emotion disappears when the
face is hidden, the pose is too weak; if it disappears when the body is hidden, the face is too weak.

### 32. Hands tell stories
Hands almost always have a purpose — holding, opening, pointing, pulling, pushing, gripping, letting
go, reaching, dropping, clenching. Avoid empty floating hands and arms hanging with nothing to do,
unless the hanging arms *are* the emotion (a beaten character). Vocabulary: `emotion-and-performance.md`.

### 33. Every pose is specific
Bad: standing, shrugging, pointing at nothing. Good: leaning over the table, yanking the charger out,
hugging the laundry basket, stepping back from the door. Every pose belongs only to this moment.

### 34. No acting for the audience
Characters live inside their world; the camera observes. No presenter poses, no looking into the
camera. Eye contact happens **between characters** — and it's one of the strongest tools there is.

### 35. Continue actions
If the previous frame opened a box, this one usually looks inside it. Continue the same place when
the moment continues; continuity travels with the characters, the objects and the time of day.

### 36. Micro-movement creates life
Eyes move, a head tilts, a letter lowers, a chair swivels, a door opens further. The scene feels alive
without rebuilding everything.

### 37. Every prop has a job
Every object teaches, supports or advances the story. If removing it changes nothing, remove it.
Props are mature — "we are not creating a kids channel": no hearts, stars, trophies, emoji-style icons or
picture bubbles standing in for a feeling (`props-symbols-metaphors-v19.md`).
Props change state instead of being replaced: closed notebook → opening → open. A recurring prop is
locked with a countable build spec (six strings, not "a guitar") — `constants.md`.

### 38. The viewer follows attention
The viewer looks where the characters look. Use gaze to guide the eye instead of arrows or labels.

### 39. Build scenes like Lego
Layer 1 hero → 2 interaction → 3 supporting objects → 4 setting → 5 background details. Build from the
inside out. Layer 4 is the very light neutral ground and, only when the place must be shown, its outline
cue ("one door outline, one bed outline, one chair, one object is enough"); layer 5 is empty.

### 39.5 What makes a frame alive
Something is happening. Thomas: *"The scene should not simply exist. Something should happen inside
the scene."* Objects moving or falling, a character reacting to something in that same frame, a
situation escalating, the scene transforming, an unexpected comparison, two states in strong contrast,
a sudden change of perspective, a small funny beat. See `aha-humour-popins.md`.

### 40. The final question
Would a professional storyboard artist draw this exact image, or simplify it? Usually they'd simplify
the elements — and push the emotion, the camera distance or the scale harder. Remove things; never remove
energy.

## PART 3 — VISUAL IDENTITY

### 41. The visual DNA
Hand-drawn 2D stick-figure explainer, emotional and cinematic. Directions Thomas named: **AxenCool**
(primary — hands, posture, distance, small gestures carrying real emotion; intentional colour),
**Sticko Explains** (simplicity, readability, parenting topics), **PsychToons** (hooks, pacing,
viral storytelling). Analyse their strengths; never copy. Never put a channel name inside a prompt.

### 42. Consistency over perfection
Never redesign a character mid-video. A viewer should never notice where one generated image ends and
the next begins. Thomas: *"Simple and inconsistent are two completely different things."* Every
recurring character **and object** looks identical in the first frame and the last.

### 43. The cast
There's no mascot. Each video has a family — often MOM and SON, plus any character the story needs
(interaction grows, so the cast grows with it), each designed by the method in `cast-design-v19.md`:
copied from the MOM or SON reference, changing only hair, size/age and small accessories where needed; never bald
or a featureless round head; a hair outline no one else has. What stays fixed across all videos is the
construction, the proportions, the face style and the quality. Teens, adults and children are instantly
distinguishable by height, build, head proportion and head details (`emotion-and-performance.md`).

### 44. Characters act the narration
They're always doing something with an objective — trying, failing, avoiding, reaching, holding on,
letting go. They never stand in a scene while the narration explains everything. Thomas, 5 Oct: "The
characters should not just 'stand in the scene.' They should clearly react." Every performance names
eyes, eyebrows, mouth, head position, hand gesture, posture and eye direction
(`acting-and-interaction-v19.md`).

### 44.6 Expressions — stronger than life, with a ceiling
Thomas: exaggerate *"slightly more than in real life — not cartoonishly extreme, but strong enough
to immediately communicate the emotional state,"* and on big moments *"intentionally exaggerated."*
Scale up the **features** (steeper brows, pupils pushed further, bigger mouth shapes), never the
**distortion**: no bared or gritted teeth, no snarl, no bulging eyes, no warped face. Every instruction
to exaggerate carries this ceiling in the same breath, or the generator finds the ugliest reading.

### 44.85 Construction rules found through production testing
All apply globally, to every frame with a character. Their exact wording lives in `constants.md`.
- **44.86 Eyebrows** are two short straight dashes with zero curvature. Only tilt, height and gap
  change — and for Thomas they change strongly (pressed low in anger, lifted high in surprise, steep
  inward tilt in frustration). Brow line weight matches the mouth.
- **44.87 No camera-facing gaze.** Attention always goes to something in the scene.
- **44.88 Eyes stay readable.** Each eye is a big white circle with a small offset pupil; the pupil is
  always visible with white around it, never cropped or lost. A narrowed eye gets one straight
  upper-lid line across the top of the circle, pupil still visible below it.
- **44.89 Human-plausible poses.** Everyday, grounded, physically believable — dramatic through
  camera and expression, never through acrobatics.
- **44.9 Complete furniture.** Any furniture a character uses has a full top and legs to the floor; a
  seated character has a seat. It is drawn as a complete, closed thin soft-grey outline with white fill —
  simple, never incomplete, never coloured.
- **44.91 Flat colour, no light effects.** No gradients, shading, glow, light rays or blur. "Lit up"
  is shown with flat colour, a bolder outline, size, or a few short ink dashes around the object.
- **44.915 Expression variety.** Vary brow tilt, brow gap and mouth shape frame to frame; never
  reuse one default face.
- **44.916 Content "printed on a page" renders as a cover.** A small book or sheet in a character's
  hand can't carry readable page content; the generator paints it as cover art. When the content
  matters, show it as its own object-only close-up (a big sheet filling the frame), then return to
  the character for the reaction.
- **44.92 Proportions from the references.** Heads exactly as in the MOM and SON reference images —
  MOM's about one-fifth of her height, SON's about one-quarter; never enlarged beyond that for
  emphasis. A big face is a camera move, not a bigger head. (The old one-seventh rule fought the
  reference images and caused head-size drift.)
- **44.93 Minimal clothing.** Bare stick bodies; only small accessories locked in a ROLE (tie, scarf,
  cap, beanie, glasses), worn in every frame, with no shirt or collar drawn around them.
- **Mouth always drawn**, single-stroke limbs, no cast shadows, no body clothing, hands always
  closed mitten loops — see `constants.md`.

### 45. Every character has an objective
A viewer should understand what the character is trying to do before hearing the narration.

### 46–47. Silhouette and recognition
Each character stays recognisable as a black silhouette, small, from behind, in profile — through
hairstyle, height and build, never through facial details alone. Every character has hair with its own
outline; never bald, never a featureless round head (`cast-design-v19.md`).

### 48. Visual simplicity
No unnecessary lines, textures, realistic wrinkles or folds. Readable at thumbnail and phone size.
Large readable shapes over small decorative details.

### 49. The drawing style — organic, not vector
Thomas asked, twice, for less "template" feeling: slightly irregular outlines, slightly imperfect
furniture, organic line quality, intentionally illustrated. That hand-drawn feel lives in the
**quality** of the ink, never in the **path** of a stroke: limbs stay single smooth strokes. Asking
for "wobbly lines" and "one clean stroke" together produced scribbled, tangled legs in Video 2.

### 49.5 Shape language
Round shapes feel friendly and safe; square shapes stable; sharp shapes dangerous. Characters are
soft rounded shapes; threats, pressure and warnings lean sharper.

### 50. Line quality
Confident lines, no sketchy construction lines, no multiple overlapping outlines, no rough pencil or
textured brush. Finished, while still hand-drawn.

### 50.5 Settings
Backgrounds stay simple: the very light neutral ground (or pure white), and — only when the place must
be shown — the one outline cue that says where we are (a door, a bed, a chair, a bus-stop post), drawn
as a thin soft-grey line with white fill, with one thin ground line in wider shots. Thomas, 5 Oct: "We do
not need to fully illustrate every location… The story should stay focused on the characters." No
decorative scenery, no tinted walls, no coloured furniture. A generator fills any *named* place with
stereotypical decor (clocks, posters, globes, plants), so settings are described by their elements,
never by the place's name, and everything not named is excluded. Thomas: *"The environment should
support the emotion, not compete with it."* Full-colour fields are only for planned emotional peaks and
night (`style-and-colour-v19.md`).

### 51. Visual language
Objects are simplified, never abstract; recognisable at a glance. For metaphors, pick objects
whose physics everyone already knows — sand escaping a fist, a rope going slack, a kettle coming to the
boil, coins filling a jar, falling dominoes. Never an invented object that has to be taught first.

### 52. Visual clarity
Never sacrifice clarity for style. The viewer never pauses to identify an object.

### 53. Recurring objects — a new meaning every time
A recurring object keeps its design exactly (consistency), but it never returns with the same meaning.
Thomas, 5 Oct: *"Recurring visual ideas are good… every recurring object should: have a reason; evolve
with the story; mean something different later; support the emotional progression. It should not return
only because it looks good."* Each return also gets a new picture. Callbacks from early segments at the
end are one of the things Thomas praised in Video 1. Reuse is not the same as inventing a recurring
abstract system; that he rejected. Plan them as MOTIFS with an arc of states and what each state means
(`story-structure.md` §4, `props-symbols-metaphors-v19.md`).

### 54. One visual language
No mixing of realistic illustration, 3D, watercolour, comic shading, painterly work or photo cut-outs
inside a video. Thomas, 5 Oct, on real objects: "let's leave real objects out… keep refining and
strengthening our own visual language." On-screen words keep one consistent style, added in Premiere
(`humour-text-contrast-v19.md`): no random fonts, styles or effects.

### 55. Simplicity is a feature
Simple drawings are the identity of the channel. Simplicity of **elements** — never simplicity of
emotion, colour or scale.

## APPENDIX — frame checklist
□ What the viewer should feel is named (`ft`) before anything else is decided
□ One visual idea, one hero, readable at a glance
□ The feeling is visible: eyes, eyebrows, mouth, head position, hand gesture, posture, eye direction
□ Sound-off test and pause test pass
□ Story moved forward; something happens inside the frame
□ Camera size and angle stated; the same character changes size or angle from the frame before; the
  composition is not one already used
□ A close-up makes sense: a reason, the place known, the gaze matched, the head whole with lead room
□ Insight shown, not the line's noun
□ Expression pushed, under the ceiling (no teeth, snarl, bulging eyes, distortion)
□ Eyebrows straight dashes; pupils placed with intent; mouth drawn
□ Hands doing something; every arm ends in a mitten loop
□ No camera-facing gaze, no presenter pose, no idle face-off
□ Teen clearly shorter than adult; child clearly a child
□ Two or more characters: an action and a visible reaction; distance stated and planned
□ One colour element (or none on a face frame); white or very light neutral ground; places as outlines
□ No strong red unless danger/warning
□ Only named objects; furniture complete as outlines; no clutter, texture, background people or
  childish symbols
□ Recurring objects restated with colour, size and counted details
□ No text in the image; on-screen idea words are added in Premiere
□ No shadows, gradients, glow, blur
□ Prompt free of instruction-shaped wording
