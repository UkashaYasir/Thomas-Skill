# The director's framework — how every decision is made (brought up to v19)

> **v25 — lookup only.** `v24-standard.md` and `RULES-CARD.md` win over everything below, then `v19-principles.md`.
> Where this file says otherwise, the current rule is: the CLEAN ground is the clean warm white #F7F6F3 and set pieces
> are thin **black** ink lines with white fill (never soft grey), the fewest the frame needs; the colour focus is bright;
> at most two coloured objects (`ce`, `ce2`); colour by meaning (red = conflict, frustration, stress, danger; phones
> purple); white by default, and an intense line turns the whole stage to its emotion's colour (`pc`, `v24-standard.md`
> §2a); words are short, playful and hand-lettered **inside the frame's own image prompt** — never added in Premiere,
> never numbers or titles; drawn glow and action marks are allowed; "larger heads" means sudden close-ups.

> **v19:** `v19-principles.md` is the law and wins over anything here. This framework is how its rules are applied, frame
> by frame. Detail: `shot-plan-v19.md` (plan and ideas), `style-and-colour-v19.md`, `camera-and-closeups-v19.md`,
> `acting-and-interaction-v19.md`, `everyday-situations-v19.md`, `props-symbols-metaphors-v19.md`,
> `humour-text-contrast-v19.md`.

This governs every step and every choice in this skill: story, ideas, scenes, shots, angles, motion, edits, sequences,
metaphors, objects, places, colour, white space, contrast, text, humour, transitions, pacing and review.
Rules elsewhere in this skill are starting points read through this framework — never quotas, never blanket rules, and
never numbers for creative choices.

## Why: how Thomas thinks
Across every video his notes come back to seven wants: the story must feel like it is moving; the eye must know where
to look; emotion lives in faces and contact; ideas must explain the line, not decorate it; clean but alive; variety and
surprise; and he judges the feel of the finished video, not rules. He also corrects extremes — "too plain" led to
clutter, "more white" led to all-white, "a colour script" led to too much colour, "more movement" led to an edit on
189 of 235 frames, which he had warned against ("not every scene needs it; simple scenes stay simple"), and "characters
must stand out" led to a soft tint on every room, which he rejected on 5 Oct 2026 ("completely built around one color
tone"). So read what he means, not only what he said: every note is a dial set by the moment, never a switch turned all
the way up — and once he states a direction (5 Oct: white by default, closer camera, behaviour not symbols), implement
it exactly as he said it, across the whole script: no overdoing, no undoing.

## 1. The director's read — before any scene or prompt
Read the whole script once as a viewer and decide: the emotional curve (where it builds, peaks and releases); the
signature images people will remember (everything else supports them); where the story moves and where it should
stand still; where we must see a face, where an idea, where the place. Only then write the shot plan
(`shot-plan-v19.md`) — Thomas: "define the emotional beats, camera angles, reactions, and visual contrasts very clearly
before production starts."

## 2. The four questions — asked for every frame and every choice
1. Feeling: what is the viewer supposed to feel here? (Thomas: "Only after that should we decide: background, prop,
   color, camera angle, movement." This is the plan's `ft` field, required on every frame.)
2. Purpose: what must the viewer understand here?
3. Focus: what one thing must the eye land on? ("one strong object, one strong face, one strong gesture")
4. Change: what is different from the previous image — and has this composition been used anywhere before?
Each frame carries one line of "why" (b.why). A choice without a reason is the wrong choice.

## 3. Moment types — starting treatments, overridden by the four questions
Seed examples, not a menu: a moment that is not listed gets its treatment from the same questions.

| Moment | Background (v19 mood) | Colour element | Space and shot | Motion | On-screen word |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Hook / opening | CLEAN or WHITE, high contrast | one bold object | a strong close-up and a frame that shows where we are | quick | when an idea word lands hard |
| Everyday story | CLEAN: the place's outline cue on the light ground | the one object that matters; everything else uncoloured | close, face + hands, reaction shots, a wider frame only to place us | a sequence only if cause → effect | rarely |
| Anger / chaos peak | WHITE behind a face close-up; a PEAK field (`pk: true`) only on a wider peak frame | none; the face carries it | extreme close-up, large face | snap or shake on the word | when the word names what the moment means |
| Hurt / loneliness | WHITE or CLEAN emptiness | none or one small object | small figure in empty space, or a large face | hold still | sometimes (SHAME, NOT REJECTION) |
| Explanation / idea | CLEAN or WHITE | only the concept object | whatever carries the idea — a real moment, a face, an object close-up, a metaphor — never a default medium/wide | the cut is the motion | an idea word at its strongest moment |
| Turn / aha | strip back to WHITE + one colour | the revelation object | held beat or match cut | hold, then cut | often, the realisation word |
| Tenderness / aftermath | CLEAN or WHITE | low | close-ups, face + hands, two people close | hold | rarely |
| Memory / the past | MEMORY: faded light grey | muted; one memory object | medium, a little distant | slow | no |

## 4. Motion: edits, sequences, stillness
Add an in-scene edit only when the line describes a change or action, the emotion turns inside the line, or a
punchline needs a beat. Do not add one when the line is a statement or explanation (cut to a new image — the cut is the
motion), when it is an emotional hold (stillness gives weight), or in a list of short lines (each line its own image).
A short sequence of stills only for a mini-story with visible cause and effect at a key emotional or turning moment —
not every scene. A frame may be still on purpose: mark it b.still with its reason; a camera move or a hold
can carry it. Camera moves follow the beat (push = tension, snap = punchline, pan = travel, tilt = scale, hold = quiet).

## 5. Shot size and angle — chosen by the moment (detail: `camera-and-closeups-v19.md`)
Thomas: "Bring the camera closer"; "Important emotional lines should visually feel important"; "avoid staying too long in
the same medium/wide framing." Extreme close-up and large face when the emotion is the information (fear, hurt,
defiance, realisation) — at the peak of an emotional cluster, never sprinkled evenly. Face + hands when the hands carry
the feeling. Reaction shot for the listener's answer to what was said or done. Close-up for dialogue, reactions and
contact. Medium for action with objects and interaction. Wide for placing us, loneliness, distance, scale, or a metaphor
world that needs room. Insert (hands, object) when the object carries the story at that moment. Every close-up makes
sense: it has a reason, the viewer knows where we are, the gaze matches the person or thing established before, and the
head shape stays whole with open space on the side the eyes look. The same character in back-to-back frames changes
size or angle clearly; inside one scene characters keep their screen sides. Eye level by default; child height when the
child's world or the power gap matters; a little above when someone feels small or overwhelmed; over the shoulder for
conversation and confrontation; side view for comparing two things. Natural angles only (Muhammad).

## 6. Literal picture or metaphor — and strong vs weak (detail: `props-symbols-metaphors-v19.md`)
Literal when the line describes behaviour we can simply show — real everyday moments carry the everyday lines. Metaphor
only where it adds meaning the words don't already carry; not every line needs one. Love, trust, attention and emotional
safety are shown through behaviour (a parent quietly sitting next to the teen, a hand reaching out, a parent kneeling to
the child's level), never through a symbol (`acting-and-interaction-v19.md`). A metaphor must pass these tests: it
explains the mechanism, not just the topic; it reads at a glance without the voice-over; it is emotionally true to the
moment; it belongs to the family's world (home, school, playground, car, weather, the story's own motifs); it is mature —
no hearts, stars, trophies, emoji-style icons or picture bubbles standing in for a feeling ("we are not creating a kids
channel"). Weak signs: stock icons (lightbulb, scales, ticks), a label or decoration, something that only works with the
narration, something random. Explore every direction; drop the most obvious.

## 7. The middle point — not too simple, not too random
Anchor every idea to the story's spine and motifs, the family's everyday world, and the chapter's one big idea.
Surprise comes from twisting familiar things (the kettle starting to boil as MOM reads the message), not from unrelated worlds.
A scene builds to its surprising image; the frames around it support it.

## 8. Colour, white space, contrast, variety, text — read from the moment (detail: `style-and-colour-v19.md`, `humour-text-contrast-v19.md`)
- Background: "white or very light neutral backgrounds as the default" — CLEAN (the clean warm white #F7F6F3, places as
  the fewest thin black ink outlines with white fill — v24) or WHITE (pure white for face-only, object-only, word frames, white breaks). This
  includes everyday story scenes and a scene's first frame: the place is shown by its outline cue, never by a tinted room.
- Colour: the colour focus (`ce`), bright by default — the important object or the part of it that matters — and a second
  object (`ce2`) only when the moment needs both; or none when the face carries the frame. Everything else is uncoloured.
  Never colour to fill space.
- Full colour: the intensity ladder (`v24-standard.md` §2a) — every line that carries the emotional intensity turns the
  stage to its emotion's colour (`pk` + `pc`), and night is NIGHT; never decoration, never a share of frames. Never a
  full-colour field behind a face close-up.
- Contrast is a family, not only colour: "empty background vs strong object; neutral scene vs one bright color; wide shot
  vs extreme close-up; serious moment vs humorous reaction; still moment vs movement; small prop vs large face; silence
  vs strong visual beat." Each frame names the contrast it makes (`cx`). The colour element always differs from its
  surroundings in hue and brightness (no brown on brown).
- Variety comes from camera distance, faces, composition, contrast and pattern interrupts — the video never settles into
  one look. Pace follows the emotion: more switches through calm explanation, held related frames at the peaks.
- On-screen words: idea words from the line (TRUST, SAFE, LISTEN, SHAME, MISREAD, NOT REJECTION…), "only at strong
  moments, not constantly", on the spoken word, short, playful and hand-lettered inside the frame's own image prompt (v24). They may sit on an emotional
  moment when the word names what it means. Never chapter numbers. No count target.

## 9. Interaction, objects, places, humour
Interaction: every frame with more than one person shows an action and a visible reaction (`ia`); eye contact or its
avoidance is clear; distance is planned (`dist`) — apart for conflict, close for repair, touch where it pays off.
Objects: recognisable, mature, simple, with the few structural details that make them that object, a job in the action
and a reason for every return — "evolve with the story, mean something different later, support the emotional
progression." Places: one outline cue and a fixed layout ("one door outline, one bed outline, one chair, one object is
enough"); nothing else, never generic clutter. Humour: human and relatable, never childish — an exaggerated parent
reaction, a "seriously?" look, an awkward pause, a parent overthinking something simple, a say/do contradiction, a short
funny reaction before returning to the serious topic — wherever the tone allows, landing on the situation or on the
parent, never on the child's pain.

## 10. The final check — watch it the way Thomas does
Before any batch: look at each chapter's frames as one strip, then the cut in motion, and ask: does it feel like a story
moving? Is anything forced? Where does the eye go? Is anything generic, repeated or random? Does any composition come
back? Do the faces clearly react? Fix before rendering.

## 11. Reading every new note from all sides
For each new client note write down: what it means, why he wants it, when it applies, when it does not, how to decide
the grey cases, and what would be the extreme he will correct next. Only then does it become a rule.

## Checks: rules vs information
Rule checks (must pass): characters on model; contrast; natural angles; no baked-in text; edit wording rules; cue words in
their lines; place layout and screen sides consistent inside a scene; the v19 rule checks (every frame has `ft`;
multi-character frames have `ia` and `dist`; close shots with a face have `look` and `ctx`; full colour only with `pk`
or NIGHT; no tinted walls or coloured furniture on CLEAN frames; no symbol props; no composition repeated). Everything
else the scripts print — shares of shot types, moods, metaphors, words, edits — is information to judge with the four
questions, never a target. Do not change a frame only to move a number.
