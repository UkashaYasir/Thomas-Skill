# Humour, on-screen words, contrast and pattern interrupts (v19)

> **v24 (9 Oct 2026) — `v24-standard.md` §7 wins over §2 of this file:** words are short, playful, emotional and
> hand-lettered ("WAIT…", "ENOUGH!", "SAFE", "TRUST", "PRESSURE", "WHY?", "INVISIBLE", "AHA!"); never numbers, section
> titles, underlines, a cold presentation font or textbook terms; readable on a phone. They may be lettered into the image
> (`tx` or `kw`) — **always inside the frame's own image prompt, never a separate image** (Muhammad, 9 Oct); a WORD frame
> is a white frame with the word lettered in. Humour and exaggeration
> are "essential parts of our channel identity" (Thomas, Video 08) — exaggerated reactions and impossible scale are in.

Read with `v19-principles.md` §5. Where `aha-humour-popins.md`, `archive/colour-and-text-v17.md` or `motion-and-energy.md` say
something different about humour, words, contrast, interrupts or pacing, this file wins.

**This is an open library.** Every section is seed examples plus a method for making new ones. Never a menu, never a
count. When a line needs a joke, a word, a contrast or an interrupt that is not here, build it with the method. After
every video, add what worked.

## 1. Humour

### 1.1 What Thomas asked for (verbatim)
- "We also need more humor, but not childish humor. Use small relatable moments like: exaggerated parent reaction;
  teenager giving a "seriously?" look; awkward pause; parent overthinking something simple; visual contradiction between
  what someone says and what they actually do; short funny reaction before returning to the serious topic. This gives
  the video more personality and keeps it entertaining."
- "Humor can also be pushed a bit more, but in a smart and subtle way. Not childish, but human and relatable."
- The summary he approved: "Small, smart humour like a "seriously?" look or a parent overthinking something."

**What the last video did** (R2 §7; measurements, not targets): about half the humour beats were gags and set-pieces
built on props (the cousin on a podium, a golden pillow, a whiteboard swung out of the window); the other half were the
subtle human kind Thomas listed. The awkward pause never appeared; the teen's "seriously?" look at a parent never
appeared; some whole chapters had no humour at all. The subtle beats were the best: "Really?" with the eyebrows jumping
while the eyes stay on the phone; MOM's deadpan "Ah yes."; DAD's sheepish shrug on "Oops."; MOM's keys hanging from her
own bun; SON's one-eyebrow side-eye; the muddy footprints with MOM's hands up and SON frozen on the last print; the shared
laugh at the lopsided cake.

### 1.2 The rules
- **Recognition, not gags.** The funny is the shared, familiar situation parents and teens know. Never the child's pain
  or the parent's struggle. Keep humour away from lines where someone is hurt, frightened or in danger.
- **Setup → held beat → reaction.** The setup shows the situation. The held beat is a still with no pop-in and no
  camera move — the pause lets the viewer see it coming (comic timing, S18). The reaction is the face, often a CLOSE or
  REACTION frame on white. The reaction carries the joke as much as the setup does (S4).
- **The picture can contradict the fixed line.** The voice-over never changes; the frame shows the truth under it. Over
  "most parents stay calm", MOM's one eye narrowed and her mitten gripping the dishwasher door far too hard. Over "I'm fine",
  the untouched bowl.
- **Understatement reads adult.** A small reaction to a big moment: one raised eyebrow, a flat mouth, a slow blink
  (an edit). When the beat is an exaggerated parent reaction, push it through a large face on white — the expression
  grows, the effects do not.
- **Exaggeration, not cartoon effects** (v24: "Humor and exaggeration are essential parts of our channel identity").
  In: exaggerated reactions and poses, impossible scale, the exaggerated cutaway (the giant helicopter), a big believable
  loss of control (the spill and the freeze, the missed shot, the thrown teddy), and drawn speed or impact lines at the
  point of action. Out: sweat drops, steam, stars, swirling eyes, anger veins, blush marks, random slapstick.
- **Fast against slow.** A quick run of stills, then one dead-still face (S18).
- **"There and back again".** A character moves on, notices, comes back; the reaction reveals who they are (S19).
- **Exits and empty frames.** The empty chair where the teen just was; the door already shut.
- **Short, then back.** Thomas's "short funny reaction before returning to the serious topic": humour is a beat, not a
  set-piece. The next frame returns to the point.
- **Spread through the video** wherever the script allows, never clustered into one set-piece and never on the painful
  lines. No count — a forced joke is worse than none.

### 1.3 Thomas's types — seeds

**Exaggerated parent reaction**
- Build: a small trigger → held beat → MOM's or DAD's large face on white: eyes wide, brows shooting up, mouth a big open
  oval, palms up or a mitten flat on the top of the body line.
- Seeds: SON says "Can we talk?" — MOM's face as if the house is on fire; a single sock on the stairs — MOM staring at it
  like evidence; SON asks DAD for a lift — DAD's huge delighted face, keys already in his mitten.

**Teenager giving a "seriously?" look** (aimed at the parent)
- Build: the parent does something embarrassing → hold → SON completely still: one brow up, one eye narrowed, a flat
  mouth, a slow blink.
- Seeds: MOM dancing in the kitchen when he walks in; DAD using a word only teenagers use; MOM waving at the school gate
  with both arms.

**Awkward pause**
- Build: two people in one frame, both loaded, nobody moving → hold → one tiny reaction.
- Seeds: MOM and SON both say "So…" at the same moment, both stop; the hug at the school gate that goes on a moment too
  long while his friends watch; "the talk" that neither starts — both on the bed edge, hands on knees, staring at the
  wall.

**Parent overthinking something simple**
- Build: a tiny trigger → MOM frozen, pupils off to one side → her overthinking shown in what she *does* (never in a
  thought bubble) → the simple answer → her face.
- Seeds: SON texts back one letter — MOM typing a long reply, deleting it, typing again (the screen's grey bars growing
  and shrinking); SON's door is shut — MOM outside it rehearsing her opening line with her hands; SON finally opens the
  door: he only wants toast.

**Visual contradiction between what someone says and what they do**
- Build: the line or the character says one thing; the picture shows the opposite in the same frame.
- Seeds: "I'm totally calm" — both mittens gripping the steering wheel; "I'm not hungry" — the empty plate and the
  crumbs; "It went great!" — one mitten pushing the test deeper into the bag; "No phones at dinner" — MOM's own phone
  lighting up beside her plate; "Really?" — eyebrows jump, eyes never leave the screen.

**Short funny reaction before returning to the serious topic**
- Build: one frame, one face, then straight back.
- Seeds: DAD's sheepish shrug on "Oops."; MOM's deadpan look straight across the table on "Ah yes."; SON's slow
  head-turn when MOM says "When I was your age…".

**More types (open):** the shared laugh (both at the lopsided cake); the empty-frame punchline; role reversal (SON
telling MOM to put her phone away); the parent caught doing the forbidden thing (MOM sneaking a biscuit at midnight meets
SON doing the same); the literal-minded young child.

### 1.4 Method: find the humour in any line
1. **Is the line safe for humour?** Not about someone's pain, fear, grief or danger. If not, skip it.
2. **What is the recognisable truth** parents and teens laugh at here — the gap between saying and doing, the parent's
   over-reaction, the teen's economy of words, the role reversal?
3. **Pick a type** (Thomas's or a new one) and the people in it.
4. **Build setup → held beat → reaction.** Decide whether the picture contradicts the line.
5. **Keep it understated:** the face, not effects; one beat, then back.
6. **Check maturity:** would a parent smile in recognition, or does it feel like a cartoon gag?
7. **Record it:** `ln` humour-aside when the line itself is an aside; `ip` funny-reaction; `cx` serious vs humorous.
   Add new seeds to §1.3 after the video.

## 2. On-screen words

### 2.1 What Thomas asked for (verbatim)
- "We are still missing some short text moments. Not too much. But selected key words can strengthen important ideas.
  Examples: TRUST, SAFE, LISTEN, TESTING, SHAME, CONTROL, MISREAD, ATTENTION, OVERWHELMED, HONESTY, NOT REJECTION. These
  should appear only at strong moments, not constantly."
- "A few strong on-screen words or short phrases to reinforce certain key points in the video. Not too much, but at the
  right moments."
- Final priorities include "intentional text moments". After an early video he named a chapter number held alone on
  screen ("The large 7 stays alone for six seconds") as its weakest moment.

**What the last video did** (R2 §8): most of its text moments were chapter labels ("NUMBER ONE", "NUMBER TWO"…), only a
few were idea words, none came before the second half, and only SAFE was from Thomas's list. Several lines spoke one of
his words — listen, attention, safe, trusting — with nothing on screen.

### 2.2 The rules
- **Idea words from the line.** The word is the line's own key word, or the one idea the line turns on. It names a
  concept the viewer should remember. Thomas's list is the model.
- **A single word or a short phrase.** Never a sentence; never the voice-over printed on screen.
- **On the spoken word.** It appears as the voice says it, next to the face or object it names, in the frame's empty
  space — never over a face.
- **Only at strong moments, not constantly:** the hook's promise, a chapter's turn, a misreading, a realisation, an
  emotional peak where the word names what the viewer must understand (SHAME on the moment of shame, NOT REJECTION as
  the boundary is held with the arm still round him), the closing message. No count.
- **Never chapter numbers as the text moment.** "Number one…" is navigation, not an idea. The keyword field holds idea
  words only; a new chapter is marked by its picture (a new place, a new state of the motif).
- *(v24.1: superseded — words are hand-lettered inside the frame's own image prompt; see the banner and `v24-standard.md` §7.)* **One consistent style, added by Muhammad in Premiere,** never drawn into the image (prompts keep their no-text rule).
  A clean cut or a soft scale-in reads as adult; a bouncing pop reads as kids' content *(our inference from "We are not
  creating a kids channel")*. Black, or the colour of the frame's colour element.
- **The word stays at least until the end of the spoken phrase** it belongs to *(our inference; a key word needs time to
  land)*.
- **The WORD frame.** A pure white frame with the word hand-lettered into it (or one small object the line names) —
  generated from its own prompt like any other frame (v24.1).
  It also works as a pattern interrupt.

**Why** (R3 §6, Mayer and Fiorella, S20):
- *Redundancy:* people learn more from picture + narration than from picture + narration + the same words on screen;
  printing the voice-over hurts.
- *Signalling:* cues that highlight the structure of the essential material help.
- *Key-word exception:* a single key word or short phrase printed next to the matching picture improved memory
  (Mayer & Johnson 2008).
- *Coherence:* decorative text costs attention.
- No credible source sets a number of words per minute, and this file sets none.

### 2.3 Seeds — word → where it fits
- **LISTEN** — MOM's mouth closes on her advice and she sits down beside him.
- **ATTENTION** — the child's drawing lowering while the parent's eyes stay on the phone.
- **TRUST** — the house key handed over; the phone screen turned toward the parent.
- **SAFE** — the payoff: the child telling the truth at the table.
- **TESTING** — the teen pushing a limit to see what happens (back late, standing in the doorway, waiting).
- **SHAME** — the test pushed deeper into the bag; both mittens over the face.
- **CONTROL** — MOM's hand reaching for the steering wheel from the passenger seat; the hand held out for the phone.
- **MISREAD** — the parent, arms akimbo, against the child's overwhelmed face.
- **OVERWHELMED** — the head sinking between the shoulders; the pile rising to the chin.
- **HONESTY** — the drawer opened, the thing put on the table.
- **NOT REJECTION** — the boundary held with an arm still round his shoulders; the parent sitting outside the shut door.
- From R2's proposals: **RECOVERY** (the gold-seamed mug — a good idea word in "Seven Things"), **DIFFERENT** (his own
  height mark), **NOT COMPETING**.
- Open: REPAIR, SEEN, PAUSE, NOT FIXING, PRACTICE, PRESSURE, BELONGING, ENOUGH — only when they are the line's idea.

**Weak words:** a word that echoes a metaphor rather than the idea (EMOTIONAL WEATHER over an umbrella); a word that means
nothing without the line (ALL THREE); numbers and chapter labels.

### 2.4 Method: choose a text moment
1. **Is this one of the strongest moments** of its chapter — the turn, the misread, the realisation, the peak, the
   message? If not, no word.
2. **Find the word:** the line's own key word, or the idea the line turns on. Prefer Thomas's vocabulary when it fits.
3. **Test it:** it names an idea (not a label, a number or the metaphor); a parent would remember it; it stands alone;
   it comes from the line.
4. **Place it:** on the spoken word, in empty space beside what it names, or on a WORD frame; never over a face.
5. **Record it** in `tx` (word, cue word, colour, place) or `kw` — both letter it into the frame's prompt — and `ip` word
   if it is also an interrupt.

## 3. Contrast

### 3.1 What Thomas asked for (verbatim)
"Contrast should not only mean black versus white. We also need contrast between: empty background vs strong object;
neutral scene vs one bright color; wide shot vs extreme close-up; serious moment vs humorous reaction; still moment vs
movement; small prop vs large face; silence vs strong visual beat. This is what keeps attention."

### 3.2 How to plan contrast
- **Every frame names its contrast in `cx`** — with the frame before, or inside the frame itself.
- **Inside a frame:** empty background vs strong object; neutral scene vs one bright colour; small prop vs large face;
  a still character vs a moving one; near vs far.
- **Across frames:** wide → extreme close-up; serious → humorous reaction; still → movement; silence → a strong visual
  beat; the very light neutral ground → a full-colour peak field.
- **Contrast raises intensity; similarity rests it** (Block's contrast and affinity, S6). Too little contrast loses the
  viewer; constant contrast wears them out. So plan where intensity rises: build toward each peak, land the strongest
  contrast there, then let it rest.
- **Make the change big.** Two shots of the same subject that differ by a little read as a mistake — a cut "neither
  subtle nor total" (S3). Change size or angle clearly.
- **The reaction shot is the contrast that gives meaning** (Kuleshov, S4): trigger → face.

### 3.3 Thomas's pairs — seeds
- **Empty background vs strong object** — the lunchbox alone on white after the front door closes; the phone face-down in
  a white field.
- **Neutral scene vs one bright colour** — the whole frame outline on white; only the cracked phone's case carries colour.
- **Wide shot vs extreme close-up** — WIDE: MOM small at the far end of the hall → XCLOSE: her eyes. The cut is the hit.
- **Serious moment vs humorous reaction** — after a heavy line, DAD's sheepish shrug; then straight back to the point.
- **Still moment vs movement** — a held still of two people not talking → SON stands abruptly, chair shoved back.
- **Small prop vs large face** — a large face filling the frame, a small test paper held up at its edge.
- **Silence vs strong visual beat** — a visual silence (held frame, no pop-in, no camera move) just before the door slam.

**More pairs (open):** open vs closed (a door, a posture); full vs empty (the chair, the plate); before vs after (the same
shot, one thing changed); what is said vs what is done; the adult world vs the child's (the same situation at work and at
school); light vs dark (CLEAN vs NIGHT); order vs mess; together vs apart (the distance script).

### 3.4 Method
1. Look at the frame before: its size, ground, colour, energy and feeling.
2. Find the change in kind that makes this frame feel different — and check that the difference carries the line's
   meaning.
3. Name it in `cx`.
4. For each chapter, find the peak: arrive at it with the strongest contrast, rest after it with affinity.

## 4. Pattern interrupts

### 4.1 What Thomas asked for (verbatim)
"The movement inside frames is better, but movement alone is not enough. We also need stronger visual switches: suddenly
white background; extreme close-up; one-word text; unexpected object; funny reaction; strong pose; short metaphor;
sudden silence visually; large facial reaction. These moments help retention."

### 4.2 The rules
- **An interrupt is a change in kind, not another cut.** New stills already arrive all the time; what counts is a switch
  to white, to a large face, to a word, to an unexpected object, to stillness.
- **Related, not random.** An extreme close-up of the character who is speaking; the same object at a new scale; the
  line's key word; a metaphor tied to the line. Unrelated rapid cuts grab the eye but are not remembered (Lang et al.,
  S23).
- **The `ip` field** names the type. Open list: white-break, extreme-close-up, word, unexpected-object, funny-reaction,
  strong-pose, short-metaphor, visual-silence, large-face. New types are allowed — for example point-of-view (what the
  child sees), empty-frame (the person just left), hands, time-jump.
- **The video never settles into one look.** No long stretch of the same kind of frame. The last video had long middle
  stretches with no hard switch at all (R2 §9).
- Interrupts vary in kind across the film; no type becomes a habit. Two of the same kind may sit close when the moment calls for it (a humour run), never as a pattern.
- **Front-load the opening.** YouTube's "Intro" measure counts who is still watching after the opening, and its advice
  is to move compelling content earlier (S22).

### 4.3 Seeds by type
- **Suddenly white background** — after outline-place frames, MOM's face alone on white as the word lands.
- **Extreme close-up** — the eyes sliding to the phone; the thumb frozen above the send button.
- **One-word text** — a WORD frame: SHAME on white, right after the mittens cover his face.
- **Unexpected object** — the car keys found in the fridge; the TV remote in the bread bin.
- **Funny reaction** — the "seriously?" look; DAD's shrug.
- **Strong pose** — SON curled on the floor with his back against the shut door; MOM in the doorway with both arms on the
  frame. A pose that reads as a silhouette.
- **Short metaphor** — one frame of the kettle boiling behind MOM; the table stretching between them.
- **Sudden silence visually** — the held still: nobody moves, no pop-in, no camera move, after the slam.
- **Large facial reaction** — a large face on white with one big, clear expression.

### 4.4 Method
1. What kind of frame has the viewer been seeing (size, ground, rhythm, energy)?
2. Is this calm explanation (interrupt more) or a peak (hold — §5)?
3. Choose the change in kind most related to this line.
4. Check it is not the same type as the last interrupt.
5. Record `ip`.

## 5. Pacing by emotion

**Research** (R3 §7): for calm messages, viewers' recognition improved as the pace rose from slow to medium to fast; for
arousing messages it peaked at a medium pace and dropped at a fast pace — "the combination of fast pace and arousing
content overloads the processing system" (Lang, Bolls, Potter & Kawahara 1999, S24). Related edits can help memory;
unrelated rapid cuts do not make it into memory (S23).

**Rules:**
- **Calm explanation → more switches.** Frequent changes in kind, pop-ins on the voice-over's words, related interrupts,
  quick real stills for a rapid list.
- **Emotional peaks → hold.** At shame, conflict or the big realisation, slow down: fewer, held, related frames — a short
  sequence of stills of one moment, one held face, a visual silence. The contrast comes from the arrival (wide → face,
  neutral → colour); then the frame holds. These are the frames marked `pk`.
- **Humour → fast against slow.** A quick run, then a dead-still face.
- **After a peak → rest** before the next build.
- **The voice-over timing is fixed.** Pacing is about what changes between stills and how much happens inside them,
  never about changing the lines.
- **`ft` decides the mode.** If the viewer should feel something big, hold. If the viewer should follow an idea, keep
  the eye moving.
