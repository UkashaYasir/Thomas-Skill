# Staging and props — every frame tells the story (brought up to v19)

> **v25 — lookup only.** `v24-standard.md` and `RULES-CARD.md` win over everything below, then `v19-principles.md`.
> Where this file says otherwise, the current rule is: the CLEAN ground is the clean warm white #F7F6F3 and set pieces
> are thin **black** ink lines with white fill (never soft grey), the fewest the frame needs; the colour focus is bright;
> at most two coloured objects (`ce`, `ce2`); colour by meaning (red = conflict, frustration, stress, danger; phones
> purple); white by default, and an intense line turns the whole stage to its emotion's colour (`pc`, `v24-standard.md`
> §2a); words are short, playful and hand-lettered **inside the frame's own image prompt** — never added in Premiere,
> never numbers or titles; drawn glow and action marks are allowed; "larger heads" means sudden close-ups.

> **v19:** `v19-principles.md` wins over anything here. Props, symbols and metaphors: `props-symbols-metaphors-v19.md`.
> Close-ups: `camera-and-closeups-v19.md`. Interaction: `acting-and-interaction-v19.md`. Thomas, 5 Oct: "one strong
> object, one strong face, one strong gesture… The viewer should always know immediately where to look."

History: Muhammad's review of the Video 4 hook renders — frames felt generic: "no object placement, no story feel, too
much weird close-up." This file was the fix; v19 keeps its intent (story in every frame, close-ups that make sense) and
drops its numbers. Read it with `story-structure.md` and `idea-not-object.md`.

## 1. Every frame answers "where is the object now?" — when there is one
The spine object's position *is* the story state: in his hand (his), held out to her (the demand), in the lockbox
(taken), on the shelf (just an object), in the charging tray (the agreement). Wherever the object is part of the moment,
place it deliberately and say where in the `action`: "THE PHONE lies face down between them", not just "THE PHONE". An
object that moves has a before and after the viewer can follow. Frames about the people — love, trust, attention shown
through behaviour — often carry no object at all; that is a strength, not a gap.

## 2. The story leads the prompt
The compiler puts the camera, the action and the objects before the construction text, so the idea is never buried.
Write the `action` so its **first sentence is the story**: who does what to whom, with which object, where. The Video 4
S2 barricade vanished because it was one clause in the middle of a long action. If a frame depends on one staged detail
(the sofa cushions as sandbags, the fuse), it goes in the first sentence, with its own PROP entry.

## 3. Places stay outlines — the story detail is the object, not the room
Thomas: "Reduce full room scenes… one door outline, one bed outline, one chair, one object is enough to communicate the
place. The story should stay focused on the characters." A place is its one outline cue on the light ground
(`style-and-colour-v19.md`). A story detail that stays put and changes state with the story (the charging shelf beside
SON's door — empty in the cold open, holding the tray later; the agreement on the fridge) is a PROP, appears only in the
frames that use it, and carries meaning each time it is shown. No room dressing, no generic props, no clutter.

## 4. Close-ups — closer, stronger, and they make sense
Thomas, 5 Oct: "We need more: close-ups, extreme close-ups, face-only moments, face + hands, emotional details.
Sometimes a large face on a white background is much stronger than a complete room." Muhammad's Video 4 note ("too much
weird close-up") is still right about *why* close-ups fail: they came with no reason and no orientation. So every
close-up makes sense (`camera-and-closeups-v19.md`):
- It has a reason: the emotional peak of the line, a reaction, or a detail the line names.
- The viewer knows where we are — a wider frame of the place came first.
- The gaze matches: the eyes look toward the person or thing established before, on the side where they are (`look`),
  and the plan says what makes the close-up make sense (`ctx`).
- The face reacts to something visible or clearly placed: hands, the object, the other person's shoulder, or the other
  face in the next frame.
- The head shape stays whole and readable, with open space on the side the eyes look. No random crops.
- Use the over-the-shoulder close-up often: the acting character's hand or shoulder in the foreground, the reacting face
  behind it — emotion and story in one frame.

## 5. Characters act on each other
Every frame with two or more people shows an action and a visible reaction (`ia`: who does what → who reacts how):
handing over, pulling, reaching for, sliding across the table, a hand on a shoulder, a door pushed shut on someone, a
parent sitting down beside the teen, a teen looking away but still listening. Say who does it and how the other reacts,
their distance (`dist`), and their eye line (met or refused).

## 6. Props and marks are used, not displayed
A prop in a character frame is held, used, looked at or reacted to — never just present. A sheet, list or calendar
carries marks (ticks, lines, one red circle), never readable words: SON adds a tick to the agreement, MOM taps the fridge
sheet, the clock's hands are what make SON get up. On-screen idea words are short and hand-lettered inside the
frame's own prompt (v24.1, `v24-standard.md` §7) — never a separate image.

## 7. Explainer lines — stage them as a real moment
When the voice explains ("the consequence can become bigger than the behaviour"), stage it first as a real everyday
moment parents and teens recognise (`everyday-situations-v19.md`), with the place shown only by its outline cue. Use a
metaphor only where it adds meaning the words don't carry, and keep a character reacting inside it. Never a floating
diagram, label or icon standing in for a reaction.

## 8. Minimal, but detailed where the eye goes
Few elements, each finished and specific: the phone has its camera dot and a switched-on screen, the lockbox has its
exact padlock count, the clock shows its hands at the exact time. Detail goes on the hero and the story objects;
backgrounds stay white, with thin black ink outline cues only where the place must be shown (v24).

## 9. No unfinished scenes, emotion all the way through
Every segment reaches a result, every problem gets a reaction, every setup pays off. The cold open drops the viewer into
the conflict; after it, the emotion never drops out for long — quiet explanation stretches still carry a feeling on the
faces — until the ending's cautious closeness. Pace follows the emotion: more switches through calm explanation, held
related frames at the peaks.
