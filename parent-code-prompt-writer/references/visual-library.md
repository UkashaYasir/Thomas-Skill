# Visual library — choose the device, don't reach for the preset

Muhammad, after the Video 4 hook renders: the scenes feel generic — no explainer direction, no story
context, the same few presets (the orange living room from the front, the navy hallway, a plain colour
field, a big face). This file is the wider vocabulary. For every line, ask **what the viewer has to
understand**, then pick the device that makes it understood — and log it in the beat's `device` field
so the QA can see whether a segment keeps reaching for the same one.

> **v19:** `v19-principles.md` wins over anything here. Every list in this file is **seed examples plus a method** — when
> a line needs a device, view or picture that is not listed, invent it the same way, and add it here after the video.
> Places are outline cues on the light ground (`style-and-colour-v19.md`); variety comes from camera distance, faces,
> composition, contrast and interrupts, not from more rooms or room colours (`camera-and-closeups-v19.md`,
> `humour-text-contrast-v19.md`); real moments and behaviour come first (`everyday-situations-v19.md`,
> `acting-and-interaction-v19.md`).

## 1. The explainer question
Before staging a line, answer in one sentence: *what must the viewer understand here that they didn't
a frame ago?* — the situation, a cause, a pattern, a feeling's size, a choice, a consequence, time
passing. The answer picks the device. A reaction shot is a device too, but it is the answer to "how
does he feel?", not to "why?" or "what is going on?". A scene that only answers "how does he feel?"
frame after frame feels generic.

## 2. Device library
Each: **KEY** — what it explains — how to stage it in this style.

**Context and situation**
- **CONTEXT** — where, when and what's at stake: the place's outline cue, the time cue and the object
  that matters (the blank homework, the clock, the phone in hand) together with the characters. A scene
  shows this before its close shots need it, so every close-up makes sense; it may open on a face when
  the face is the hook and the place follows.
- **CUTAWAY_MAP** — the whole situation at once, drawn as outlines: a floor plan from above, the house as
  a simple outline cross-section with each character in their room. Use when the line talks about "the
  house", "the family", "everywhere", or spread and distance.
- **THROUGH_FRAME** — being inside or outside a situation: shoot through a doorway, a door gap, a
  window, the space under a table, the bars of a stair rail. Perfect for being shut out or watching.
- **AFTERMATH** — what's left after the event: the empty room, the chair pushed back, the object
  left on the table. Lets a feeling land without a face.

**Escalation, pattern and time**
- **ESCALATION_RAMP** — a sequence that gets worse: across a few frames the camera steps closer and the
  faces get stronger while one thing stays fixed (her open palm); at the top, a PEAK colour only if it is
  a planned emotional peak. Reads as stages without numbers.
- **CONSTANT_VS_CHANGE** — something stays while everything else moves: the same object in the same
  spot while the characters change around it; the same hand held out while the reaction escalates.
- **REPEAT_WITH_VARIATION** — a pattern ("phone gone" again and again): the same framing device
  returns, one detail escalates each time (one more padlock) and the camera changes. The joke and the
  point are the variation.
- **TIME_MARKER** — time passing: the clock's hands, the day turning to NIGHT, the same place at two
  times across two frames.
- **CAUSE_EFFECT_CUT** — this leads to that: frame A the cause (3 a.m. scrolling), frame B the effect
  (asleep at school), same character, different place, the link unmistakable.
- **BEFORE_AFTER** — a change: two frames with the same framing, one thing different.

**Size and inner world**
- **SCALE_SHIFT** — how big something feels: the object grows (the phone as tall as a wall) or shrinks
  (the homework tiny at his feet) with its meaning.
- **OBJECT_POV** — the object's side of it: the camera looks up from the phone's screen at the two
  faces over it, or from inside the lockbox as the lid closes.
- **INSERT_DETAIL** — the object's state tells the story: a tight shot of the blank homework under the
  tug, the padlock count, the empty charging slot.
- **METAPHOR_OBJECT** — a physical object stands for the idea inside a real scene: the fuse on the
  phone, the kettle coming to the boil, the puzzle piece.
- **METAPHOR_WORLD** — the scene moves into a metaphor place: the iceberg, the river of stepping
  stones, the fork in the road — with a character reacting inside it.
- **SILENT_BEAT** — visual silence (one of Thomas's pattern interrupts): a quiet held face, often alone
  on white, after a loud moment; the place and the cause were shown before.
- **FACE_ONLY** — a large face on white: "Sometimes a large face on a white background is much stronger
  than a complete room." The gaze points toward the person or thing established before, with open space
  on that side.
- **FACE_HANDS** — the face and the hands that carry the feeling in one frame.
- **REACTION_SHOT** — the listener's face answering what was just said or done; the interaction's second
  half.
- **WORD_FRAME** — a pure white frame for an on-screen idea word, added in Premiere.

**Relationship and power**
- **BEHAVIOUR** — love, trust, attention or safety shown as a real behaviour: a parent quietly sitting
  next to the teen, a hand reaching out, a parent kneeling to the child's level, a teen looking away but
  still listening (`acting-and-interaction-v19.md`).
- **EVERYDAY_MOMENT** — a moment parents and teens recognise at once: "I'm fine" when he isn't, too many
  questions, the eye roll, the parent who walks past, notices and comes back (`everyday-situations-v19.md`).
- **OTS_REACTION** — action and reaction in one frame: the acting character's hand or shoulder in the
  foreground, the reacting face behind it.
- **POWER_ANGLE** — who has the power: low camera on the one in control, high camera on the one losing
  it; flip the angles when the power flips.
- **DISTANCE** — the relationship as space: how far apart they stand, a table or a doorway between them,
  the gap closing across a scene.
- **MIRROR** — imitation or recognition: one character copying the other's posture, or pulling the
  other's exact face.
- **TUG_OF_WAR** — a power struggle over one object: two pairs of hands, one object, tension dashes.
- **SPLIT_STATE** — two sides of one moment: a wall cutaway showing both rooms, a split frame with one
  character on the centre line (never the same character twice).

**Explaining and structuring**
- **HYPOTHETICAL** — "imagine…", "maybe…", "suppose…": a clear shift into the imagined scene — a
  change of place (for example the office for the adult analogy), no dotted outlines.
- **LIST_DEVICE** — steps and lists: a recurring framing (the fridge sheet, the stepping stones) that
  changes each time it returns — a new angle, a new mark, a new state; never five identical inserts.
- **CALLBACK** — the earlier image returns changed: the demand from frame 1 at the end, the war-zone
  table as the battlefield, the same shelf now holding the tray.

## 3. Place views — the camera moves inside one outline place
Thomas: "We do not need to fully illustrate every location. Sometimes just: one door outline, one bed
outline, one chair, one object is enough to communicate the place." A place is its outline cue on the
light ground; variety comes from where the camera stands and how close it is, not from more rooms. Seed
views to plan before the video (add new ones the same way):
- **Placing frame** — wider, the outline cue and the characters, so the viewer knows where we are.
- **Reverse** — toward the doorway or the window outline, the other character's side.
- **Through** — from outside through the doorway outline or a gap.
- **Close views** — a face, face + hands, hands, the object — usually on white, the place already known.
Natural camera positions only (eye level, a little above, child height, over the shoulder); floor and
ceiling views render badly. Inside one scene the characters keep their screen sides. A story detail that
stays put (the charging shelf) is a PROP shown only when it matters, changing state and meaning with the
story.

## 4. Colour logic (v19 — details in `style-and-colour-v19.md`)
- "White or very light neutral backgrounds as the default." The ground is CLEAN (very light neutral) or
  WHITE; places are thin soft-grey outlines with white fill.
- "Only the important object or emotional element should carry strong color" — one colour element per
  frame (the turquoise phone on white), or none when the face carries the frame.
- Full-colour fields only for planned emotional peaks (PEAK, `pk: true`) and night (NIGHT), rarely, and
  never behind a face close-up. A **hotspot** (one lit window in a NIGHT house) and a colour arriving on a
  peak are good uses of colour; colour used only to fill space is not.
- No one-frame colour flicker inside a continuous scene.

## 5. Bold metaphors — Thomas's Video 4 list (amplify the voice-over, don't illustrate it)
Since 5 Oct: "stronger visual metaphors" — used where they add meaning the words don't carry, mature, and
never for love, trust, attention or safety (those are behaviour). Standard objects (phone, table, sofa,
bed, lamp, door, clock) are fine, but the style allows much more.
Push scale, perspective and surprise; make the metaphor physical and let a character react inside it:
- **Pressure** → a huge, heavy backpack (or the punishment object itself) pulls the character down.
- **Lack of trust / secrecy** → a large wall suddenly rises between parent and teenager.
- **Phone addiction** → an oversized phone dominates the scene or pulls the character inside its screen.
- **Time pressure** → a giant clock chases or looms over the character.
- **Too many messages** → a mountain of blank message bubbles grows around the character.
- **Lifeline** → the character floats in a dark sea holding the phone like a life ring.
The transformation is usually an in-scene edit (the wall rises, the mountain grows, he is pulled in).
Fewer elements, more interesting ones: a white ground and one bold object beat a busy frame.

**Fresh visual stimulus throughout.** Thomas asked in Video 4 for regular fresh visual stimulus, never
chaotic; v19 keeps the intent without a timer: the video never settles into one look. Any of
his pattern interrupts counts — a sudden white background, an extreme close-up, a one-word text, an
unexpected object, a funny reaction, a strong pose, a short metaphor, a visual silence, a large facial
reaction — not only metaphors and transformation edits.

## 6. Variety rules
- Fill the `device` field on every beat with one KEY from section 2, or a new key made the same way.
- A segment never leans on one device; the checks print the spread as information.
- The same composition (shot + angle + place + left/centre/right layout) never repeats anywhere in the film. Thomas:
  "the viewer should still never feel like the visuals are repeating or that the same composition keeps coming back."
- The same character in back-to-back frames changes size or angle clearly; inside one scene characters keep their
  screen sides.
- The place is shown (CONTEXT, CUTAWAY_MAP or THROUGH_FRAME) before the close shots that depend on it.
- POWER_ANGLE is used sparingly: most frames sit at eye level, square-on, side-on or over the shoulder, so the few low or
  high angles mean something.
