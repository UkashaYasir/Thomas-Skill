# Emotion and performance — the channel's main rule, made concrete

> **v25 — lookup only.** `v24-standard.md` and `RULES-CARD.md` win over everything below, then `v19-principles.md`.
> Where this file says otherwise, the current rule is: the CLEAN ground is the clean warm white #F7F6F3 and set pieces
> are thin **black** ink lines with white fill (never soft grey), the fewest the frame needs; the colour focus is bright;
> at most two coloured objects (`ce`, `ce2`); colour by meaning (red = conflict, frustration, stress, danger; phones
> purple); white by default, and an intense line turns the whole stage to its emotion's colour (`pc`, `v24-standard.md`
> §2a); words are short, playful and hand-lettered **inside the frame's own image prompt** — never added in Premiere,
> never numbers or titles; drawn glow and action marks are allowed; "larger heads" means sudden close-ups.

> "Before designing a scene, ask: what should the viewer FEEL here? Then build facial expression,
> eyes, body language, hands, distance, camera framing, object and colour around that emotion.
> The characters should never feel like they are simply standing in a scene while the narration
> explains everything. They must ACT the narration." — Thomas, first sample review

After Video 3 he said the characters and expressions are *"currently one of your biggest strengths —
keep them exactly in this direction."* This file is how that level is reproduced every time.

> **v19 (Thomas, 5 Oct 2026):** "We need stronger facial expressions… Please use more: eyes, eyebrows, mouth movement,
> head position, hand gestures, posture, eye direction. The characters should not just 'stand in the scene.' They should
> clearly react." Every performance names all seven features as one clear emotion, described by what the face and body
> do, with a verb — never a label, never a static state. Behaviours, interaction and the love/trust/attention library
> are in `acting-and-interaction-v19.md`; `v19-principles.md` §3 wins over anything here.

## The build order — feeling out, never object in

1. **Feel** — name the one emotion the viewer should feel (not "sad": *hurt at being ignored*).
2. **Face** — eyes, eyebrows and the mouth shape, using the table below.
3. **Eye direction** — where exactly the pupils go: at the other character, away from them, down, up in
   an eye-roll, at the object. The eyes are never static.
4. **Head position** — tilted, lowered, turned away, lifted, pulled back.
5. **Posture** — leaning in or away, turning a shoulder, stepping back, shrinking, bracing, slumping.
6. **Hand gesture** — what the hands grip, drop, point at, push away, hide, reach for.
7. **Distance** — how far apart two characters are, and whether it's opening or closing.
8. **Camera** — the shot size and angle that make that feeling land (a large face, face + hands, a
   reaction shot).
9. **Object** — the one thing in play, if any.
10. **Colour** — the one colour element, if any; often none, because the face carries the frame
    (`style-and-colour-v19.md`).

## The ceiling — state it every time you push

Thomas wants expressions stronger than real life. The one time we pushed without a limit
("most extreme readable shape, do not moderate it") the renders came back with gritted teeth and
rage faces. So: **scale up the features, not the distortion.**
- Allowed: strongly tilted brows, brows pressed low onto the eyes or lifted high, pupils pushed hard to
  a corner, a large open oval mouth, a deep downturned frown, a tight flat pressed line, a big clean
  smile, a single straight upper-lid line narrowing an eye.
- Never: bared or gritted teeth, a snarl, bulging or popping eyes, a stretched or warped face, tears
  streaming, horror or rage caricature, a lopsided smug grin with drooping lids.
The shared `PERFORMANCE_AND_EXPRESSION` constant carries this ceiling in every prompt; the per-beat
performance text only has to name the specific shape.

## Expression table

Eyebrows are always two straight dashes; only tilt, height and gap move. Pupils are always a solid
black dot inside a big white circle, placed where the attention goes. Every mouth is a shape, never "a
small … line" (a round open circle, a wide oval, a deep downturned curve, a big wavy line, a hard flat or
tight pressed line, a clear smile). Write all seven features — eyes, eyebrows, mouth, head position,
hand gesture, posture, eye direction — for every visible face. The rows are seed examples: a feeling that
is not listed is built the same way.

| Feeling | Brows | Pupils / eyes | Mouth | Head and posture | Hands |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Angry | pressed low, steep inward tilt, almost touching the eyes | locked on the other person, lid line narrowing the top | hard flat line or open shouting oval (no teeth) | head thrust forward, leaning in, squared up | pointing, fist, slapping the table |
| Frustrated | inward tilt, knotted gap | darting away, to the side | tight pressed line, corners down | head turning aside, a shoulder turning away | rubbing the forehead, gripping the object too hard |
| Hurt | inner ends lifted, outer ends dropped | lowered to the floor, soft | deep downturned curve | head dropping, shoulders collapsing, curling inward | hand dropping, arms pulling in |
| Ignored | slightly lifted, then falling | on the other person, who isn't looking back — then away | mouth half-open, closing | energy dropping, slumping back | hand that was raised to speak lowering |
| Rebellious / dismissive | one brow higher than the other | rolled to the top of the eye circles, or sideways | flat, one corner tight | turned away, weight on back foot | hands tucked behind the back, crossed arms, a dismissive flick |
| Controlling (parent) | lowered, level and hard | fixed on the teen | firm flat line | standing tall over him, leaning in | pointing, arms akimbo, hand held out for the phone |
| Defensive | drawn together | sideways, avoiding | tight | stepping back, shoulder forward | crossed arms, palm up as a shield |
| Surprised | lifted high, wide gap | centred, circles wide open | round open "o" | pulling back, freezing mid-step | hands up, dropping what they held |
| Overwhelmed | inner ends up, pinched | darting in two directions | wavering open line | small, hunched, head sinking | hands to the head, clutching too many things |
| Afraid / worried | inner ends high, tilted | wide, looking at the threat | big wavy line | head pulled back, shrinking, leaning away | hands close to the chest, gripping a strap |
| Embarrassed | lifted in the middle | down and to the side | awkward flat squiggle | shoulders up, head ducking | hand rubbing the back of the neck |
| Realisation | rising | fixed on the object or the other person | slowly opening | stopping, straightening | hand freezing mid-action |
| Relieved | relaxing, level, lower | soft, half-down | a clear soft curve | head tipping back a little, shoulders dropping | hands loosening, opening |
| Connected / warm | level, relaxed | on each other — clear eye contact | a clear warm smile | heads tilted toward each other, leaning in | open palm, hand on a shoulder |
| Proud | level, slightly lifted | on the achievement or the child | clean symmetrical smile, eyes fully open | upright, top of the body line straight | presenting the thing, arm around them |
| Calm / calming (parent) | level, soft | on the teen | a gentle clear curve | kneeling or sitting low, at his level | open palm, slow lowering gesture |
| Exhausted | outer ends dropping | half-down, heavy lids | flat, slack | slumped, leaning on furniture | hanging arms (this is when hanging arms are right) |
| Guarded / uncertain | uneven, one slightly raised | sideways glance at the other person | tight pressed line | head half-turned, body half-turned | holding the object close |
| Hesitating before telling the truth | inner ends lifted, uneven | flicking to the parent, then down | mouth open a little, then pressing shut | head lowered, weight shifting back | a mitten hand fidgeting with an object, a hand half-raised then pulled back |
| Looking away but still listening | level, slightly drawn | turned away from the speaker, but the head tilted toward the voice | tight pressed line | shoulder toward the speaker, body angled away | still — the hands stop what they were doing |
| Pretending not to care | one brow raised, the other level | on the phone or the wall, a sideways flick toward the parent | flat, one corner tight | slouched, head turned, body still angled toward the parent | busy with an object that doesn't matter |
| Parent stopping before reacting | lifted, then lowering | wide on the teen, then softening | open oval closing to a pressed line | head pulled back, then still | a raised hand stopping in mid-air |
| Noticing the other is uncomfortable | inner ends lifting | moving from the task to the other person's face | open, soft | head turning toward them, body pausing mid-step | hands setting the task down |

A close-up never carries a blank, neutral or unreadable face. Even quiet beats have a clear feeling:
guarded, uncertain, softening, wary, tired.

## Eyes — Thomas's list, verbatim intent
Stronger eye direction. Visible eye contact. Looking away when someone emotionally withdraws.
Narrowed eyes in conflict. Softer eyes in connection. Raised brows when surprised. Lowered brows when
frustrated or controlling. **The eyes should never feel static.** Where two characters share a frame,
always say who is looking at whom — and whether it's returned.

## Face size — an explicit field on every frame
- **NORMAL** — natural scale for the shot.
- **LARGE** — head and shoulders fill about half the frame.
- **HUGE** — the camera moves in until the head alone fills most of the frame (Thomas: "Faces can
  occupy 60–80%"), the body cropped out below. The head keeps its normal proportions and its shape stays
  whole and readable, with open space on the side the eyes look.
Thomas, 5 Oct: "Bring the camera closer… Important emotional lines should visually feel important." Every
important emotional line gets a LARGE or HUGE face, wherever the emotion is — not rationed, not bunched.

**Extreme close-ups and face-only frames.** On the peak emotional lines — shock, anger, hurt, the moment
of realising — go past HUGE into `XCLOSE` (eyes, brows and mouth fill the frame; eyes-only is a crop of
it). A large face alone on pure white is a main tool: "Sometimes a large face on a white background is
much stronger than a complete room." What makes a close-up make sense (`camera-and-closeups-v19.md`): it
has a reason (the peak of the line, a reaction, or a detail the line names); the viewer already knows
where we are; the gaze points at the person or thing established before, toward the side where they are;
the face reacts to something visible or clearly placed (hands, the object, the other person's shoulder,
the other face in the next frame); the head stays whole with open space on the gaze side. No random
crops.

## Hands-only and face + hands shots
Hands-only: cropped at the forearms, the head entirely out of frame, the mitten hands and their object
across most of the frame, the forearms rising from the bottom edge (or the bottom corners for two
people). They break desk-and-table monotony better than any camera move: the phone being pulled from a
hand, a hand letting go of the rope, a hand reaching out and stopping, a hand hovering over a door
handle. The performance field describes the **hands**, never a face that isn't in the shot, and the
setting matches the camera — the very light neutral ground (CLEAN: white mitten hands need it to separate; pure white
only as a deliberate white break), or a tabletop SURFACE for an overhead shot.
Face + hands (`FACE_HANDS`): the face and the hands that carry the feeling in one frame — a hand over
the mouth, hands gripping the phone under a worried face. Thomas asked for both ("face + hands"); use
them wherever the hands carry the emotion.

## Character interaction — two people, one moment

Thomas: *"The viewer should immediately feel and understand the relationship between the characters
through facial expressions, hands, posture, eye direction, distance and interaction."* *"The people and
their emotional interaction must remain the main focus."* And 5 Oct: *"more real interaction between
characters."* A frame with two characters is never two separate portraits side by side. It's one moment
between them, planned as an interaction beat (`ia`: who does what → who reacts how) and a distance
(`dist`: touching, close, apart, far) — `acting-and-interaction-v19.md`.

- **Action and reaction in the same frame.** One character does something; the other visibly reacts
  to it right there — she holds out her hand for the phone, he pulls it back against himself; he
  starts to speak, she is already turning away. Write both halves.
- **Each character gets their own full performance** — their own brows, pupils and mouth, their own
  hand action, their own body direction. Name every character in the performance field.
- **Eyes say who is connected.** Always state who looks at whom and whether it's returned: eye contact
  met, eye contact refused, one watching while the other looks away, both looking at the same object.
- **Distance and direction** — the gap, who is higher, who is turned toward or away (see *Distance
  tells the story* below). Conflict opens the space, connection closes it.
- **Love, trust, attention and emotional safety are behaviour, never a symbol.** Thomas: "a parent
  quietly sitting next to a teenager; a teen looking away but still listening; a hand reaching out; a
  parent stopping before reacting; a child hesitating before telling the truth; someone noticing the other
  person is uncomfortable; a parent kneeling down to the child's level; two people sitting together without
  needing a heart symbol." The full behaviour library and the method for new ones:
  `acting-and-interaction-v19.md`.
- **Interaction vocabulary** (seed examples — use the one that is the story, not a default): handing over or refusing
  an object; reaching and pulling back; a hand resting on a shoulder (a mitten loop laid on the
  shoulder point); blocking a doorway; turning away mid-sentence; talking over someone (one mouth wide
  open, the other small and closing); following at a distance; standing guard; mirroring each other's
  posture; sitting down beside; leaning in or leaning away; both hands on the same object (the tug);
  a hand hovering, not quite touching.
- **Touch where it pays off.** Physical contact is a story event (the first touch after the fight, a
  hand on the shoulder at the repair) — use it wherever the moment is connection, planned in `dist`, not
  rationed. Draw it simply: a closed mitten loop resting against the other character's line, never a
  detailed grip.
- **Over-the-shoulder frames** — the back-turned character still has a performance (body and hands,
  head tilt); the facing character carries the face.
- The compiler adds an INTERACTION block to every frame with two or more visible characters; the beat
  text still has to say what the interaction *is*.

## Recognition — every character readable in half a second

Thomas: children and adults distinguished *"through size, body proportions, and, where appropriate,
also through small visual details such as a beard, mustache, glasses, or other clear identifying
features. That way, it is immediately understandable who is who."* Standard 6 adds *"clothing and
other visual characteristics"* — so clothing is **minimal**: the body and limbs are never clothed
(no shirts, jackets, dresses, trousers), but a character can wear small accessories — a tie, a bow
tie or a scarf at the neck, a cap, a beanie, a headband or glasses on the head — locked in their
ROLE and worn in every frame (`constants.md`).

Every ROLE gets a **recognition kit** that reads from the front, in profile, from behind and small in a
wide shot:
1. **Hairstyle silhouette** — the strongest marker; no two characters share one (bun vs spiky clumps
   vs short flat cap of hair vs ponytail vs tight curls vs a short side parting with a beard). Never bald,
   never "no hair", never a featureless round head (`cast-design-v19.md`).
2. **Height and build** — adult / teen / child relationships fixed and stated.
3. **One head detail or accessory**, where the family has more than one adult or more than one child
   of similar age — glasses, a beard, a moustache, a cap, a tie, a hair bow, in the character's own
   muted accent colour (Dad's tie, the younger brother's cap).
Every character has its own hair outline; family members also differ clearly in height/build or a
head detail. When two characters could be confused (two siblings, two parents), give one a head detail
in a locked colour and keep it in every frame. The
compiler adds a RECOGNITION block to wide, profile, over-the-shoulder, high, overhead and
multi-character frames — the frames where identity is hardest to read.

## Body language vocabulary
Crossed arms, shoulders turning away, stepping backwards, leaning away, pointing, raised hands,
clenched hands, hands dropping, open palms, hands tucked behind the back, arms akimbo, a hand raised
to speak, slumping onto a chair, blocking a doorway, sitting on the stairs, hugging the knees. Every
character frame says what the hands are doing.

Thomas's own lists — parent: pointing, open palm, crossed arms, arms akimbo, calming hand gesture;
teen: crossed arms, defensive hand gesture, hand raised to speak, hand dropping after being ignored.
"Hands on hips" is written as **arms akimbo** (hands planted at the sides, elbows out) — the pose
without the anatomy word, which QA flags. He also listed "hands in pockets" — the characters have no clothing, so there are no pockets; use
hands tucked behind the back or arms folded tight instead, which read the same way. Never write
"pocket", "hood", "sleeve" or any garment word into a character's action — the generator will draw
the garment. The only things worn are the accessories locked in that character's ROLE.

## Distance tells the story
**Conflict opens the space; connection closes it.** Every two-character frame states the gap and who
is where (left/right, near/far, above/below), and the gap matches the beat. Across a film the gap is a
story line of its own: wide across the kitchen at the start, one step apart at the repair, side by
side on the steps at the end. Inside one scene characters keep their screen sides (180° rule); between
scenes vary the sides and layouts so not every two-shot has the mother on the left (history: Video 1 had
her left far more often than right; Seven Things repeated it).

## Teen, adult, child — instantly distinguishable
Thomas: *"Children and teenagers should never simply look like smaller copies of the adult."*
Proportions come **exactly from the MOM and SON reference images** (`assets/characters/`) — the head
is never enlarged beyond them for expression or emphasis. A HUGE face is the camera moving closer with
the body cropped out, never a bigger head on the same body.
- **Adult (MOM's build)** — head about one-fifth of standing height (the round face alone; hair and a
  bun add more on top), the body line about one and a half head-heights, the legs about two and a half,
  hands hanging to just below the hips; head details and accessories that read adult (a bun, glasses,
  a beard, a tie).
- **Teen (SON's build)** — head about one-quarter of standing height, as in the SON reference, on a
  lean body with shorter legs than the adult; a teenage posture (slouch, weight on one leg, hands tucked
  behind), and clearly shorter than the adult, as in `mom-son-height.png`: about four-fifths of her height, his
  chin at about her shoulder.
- **Child** — a genuinely different build: head about one-fifth of standing height, short arms and
  legs, their head reaching only about halfway up an adult's height; softer, rounder hair. Proportionate for a young child — never
  an oversized bobble head.
In prompts about a teenager, avoid anatomy words (thigh, hip, waist, chest, "his body") — they read
badly to safety filters even in a stick-figure style. Use geometry: shoulder height, leg, side.

## Banned defaults
- The "deadbot": arms straight at the sides, torso square to camera, no weight shift, no task.
- Two characters standing face to face with empty hands and no motion — one of them is moving or
  using something, unless the freeze itself is the named beat (a stare-down).
- The smug combo (lopsided grin + drooping lids); for pride, a clean symmetrical smile, eyes open.
- Hand-on-chin thinking; show thinking with a head tilt, torso twist or weight shift.
- The same face in back-to-back frames.

## Cause-and-effect sequences
When the script describes a process — withdrawal, escalation, trust returning — storyboard every step
as its own frame instead of one summary image. Thomas's model sequence (Video 1, the one he called
*"exactly the kind of visual storytelling I want"*): tries to be heard → gets overruled → energy
drops → hand lowers → eyes disengage → emotionally withdraws.

## Endings
Thomas standard 8: avoid overly happy or perfect ending visuals. Relief, softer eyes, less tension in
the shoulders, cautious closeness, a calm moment. Not exaggerated happiness, not a group hug with big
smiles. Let the final frames hold a close-up on the eyes.
