# Props, symbols and metaphors (v19)

> **v24 (9 Oct 2026) — `v24-standard.md` §4 wins over this file:** important objects may be two or three times larger
> for a moment; when the voice-over names a vivid image, an **exaggerated cutaway** shows it big and bright behind the
> character ("a large exaggerated yellow or bright green helicopter… It does not always have to look completely
> realistic") — this restores the helicopter picture that v19.1 removed as a cliché; a letter on an object is allowed when
> the letter is the story (the red F). Props are bright on the colour focus, black line elsewhere (§4 of this file).

Read with `v19-principles.md` §4. Where `metaphor-bank.md`, `staging-and-props.md` or `idea-not-object.md` say
something different about props, symbols or metaphors, this file wins.

**This is an open library.** The props, replacements and metaphors below are seeds, each with a method for making new
ones (§1, §8). Never a menu. When a line needs an object or a metaphor that is not here, build it with the method and
the tests. After every video, add what worked.

## 0. What Thomas asked for (verbatim)

- "We are not creating a kids channel. • The audience is parents and teenagers. • Some symbols still feel too simple or
  childish. • For example, the smartphone with the large heart feels too basic and too much like children's content."
- "The smartphone with the heart feels more like a kids channel visual… If we want to show love, attention, or
  connection, it should come more through real situations, behavior, closeness, eye contact, gestures, and reactions,
  not through simple standard symbols."
- "This is very important. I would rather have: one strong object, one strong face, one strong gesture than: bed, chair,
  lamp, table, shelf, wall, decoration, several small props all at the same time. The viewer should always know
  immediately where to look."
- "If the phone is important, let the phone stand out." "do not fully color every object just because it exists in the
  scene."
- "Less: background, decoration, full-screen color, random props, generic symbols."
- Final priorities include "better and more mature props" and "stronger visual metaphors".
- Recurring objects: "The jar concept is a good example. I like that some visual elements return later in the video.
  Please continue doing this. But every recurring object should: have a reason; evolve with the story; mean something
  different later; support the emotional progression. It should not return only because it looks good."

**"Real objects" means two different things — keep them apart.** Thomas's answer "let's leave real objects out… keep
refining and strengthening our own visual language" is about photographic or real-image material mixed into the
drawing. That stays out. The props themselves should be *real everyday objects* — a mug, car keys, a permission slip —
drawn in our flat illustrated style.

**What the last video did** (R2 §5; measurements, not targets): a fifth of the frames carried a childish or generic
symbol, an emoji-style pop-in or a picture bubble — the heart phone, hearts, gold stars and star frames, a report card of
stars, trophies, a podium, a golden pillow, a coin tower, a smile mask, story bubbles with a dinosaur, an empty speech
bubble, a stop paddle, a heart printed on the mended mug, and sparkle, confetti, heart, question-mark, siren and sweat
pop-ins. The real everyday props (the phone, the test, the keys, the permission form, the juice glass, the skateboard)
and the adult metaphor props carried the strongest ideas.

## 1. The maturity test — every prop, before it enters the build

Ask each question. A prop that fails is replaced, not softened.

1. **Is it a real everyday object a parent recognises from their own life?** A mug, a phone, car keys, a school bag, a
   test paper, a kettle, a laundry basket, a lunchbox, a permission slip.
2. **Does it carry meaning through its use or its state** — what someone does with it, where it is, what has happened
   to it — and not through a symbol printed on it? A phone turned face-down, not a phone showing a heart.
3. **Does it have a characterful detail** that makes it this family's object? A chip on the mug's rim, a crack across
   the phone's corner, a sticker on the laptop lid, a keyring, an eraser worn down to a stub. One detail, not decoration.
4. **Colour only if it is the frame's colour element** (`ce`). Otherwise the hero object keeps a bold black outline with
   white fill, and secondary objects are thin soft-grey outlines with white fill (`style-and-colour-v19.md`).
5. **No words, numbers or logos on it.** Text is added only in Premiere. Screens show plain grey bars or one simple
   drawn picture.
6. **Would it look at home in a grown-up film or an editorial illustration?** No faces or eyes on objects, no sparkles
   or shine stars, no hearts or stars printed on things, no cartoon gloss.
7. **Does the frame need it at all?** One strong object. If removing it makes the idea clearer, remove it.

In the build, a PROP that fails carries `mature: false`, and the v19 check rejects any frame that uses it.

Why this is right (R3 §4): flat, sparse, stylised drawing is not childish in itself — UPA made adult films that way
(S17), and School of Life animators put "a single object lying in the centre of the composition" (S16). What reads as
childish is the content and the symbolism. Spot colour works because it is rare and carries meaning (S14).

## 2. Generic symbols that are out — and what replaces them

**Out:** hearts of every kind (the heart phone is Thomas's own example; heart pop-ins; hearts printed on mugs or cards);
stars (gold stars, star stickers, star frames, report cards of stars); trophies and podiums as icons of success; coin
towers and golden pillows; emoji-style icons and pop-ins (sparkles, confetti, question marks, exclamation marks,
lightning zigzags, sweat drops, alarm sirens, little rain clouds, ticks and crosses); picture bubbles standing for a
feeling (speech or thought bubbles holding drawings, scribbles or knots; an empty speech bubble for silence); smile
masks; stop signs and stop paddles; light bulbs over heads; cartoon faces on objects (a scribble monster with eyes, a
frowning iceberg).

**What replaces them:** behaviour, a real object, or the face. Seeds:

| Symbol | What it tried to say | What replaces it |
|---|---|---|
| the heart phone | secret relationships | SON's phone lights up; he flips it face-down a beat too fast; MOM's single raised eyebrow (behaviour + real object) |
| a heart | love | SON sets a mug of tea beside MOM's laptop and is turning away before she looks up; two people sitting together (behaviour) |
| a heart pop on a hug | warmth | the hug held still; MOM's eyes shut, her mouth soft (the face) |
| gold star, star frames, report card of stars | approval, achievement | a real test with the teacher's circle; a certificate held on the fridge by one magnet; the parent's face as she reads it (real object + face) |
| trophy, podium | being loved for winning | MOM filming the match on her phone; SON looking at her phone, not the ball (behaviour) |
| smile mask | "I'm fine" | the too-wide smile on the real face, a mitten scrubbing a clean pan too hard (face + behaviour) |
| empty speech bubble | silence | empty space between two people, no eye contact, only the forks moving (space + behaviour) |
| story bubble with a picture | the child telling a story | the child's mittens mid-gesture, mouth open; the parent's eyes on the phone (behaviour) |
| stop paddle, stop sign | a boundary | a calm raised flat palm at shoulder height, eyes level (the gesture) |
| question-mark pop | confusion | brows raised, head tilted (the face) |
| sweat drops | nerves | frozen posture, pupils darting (the body) |
| lightning zigzag | tension | both mouths open at once, then a held silence (interaction) |
| alarm or siren pop | the parent's panic | the head snapping up, eyes wide — a large face on white (the face) |
| sparkle, confetti | joy, pride | the face (eyes shut in curves, a big smile), or an idea word |
| light bulb | realisation | a hand freezing mid-action, eyes widening (the face) |
| stress knot, worry scribble | anxiety passed on | SON's shoulders rising to the same height as MOM's (the body) |
| frowning iceberg | "deeper than grades" | MOM's eyes going to his face, not the paper (behaviour) |

## 3. Seed mature props, by place — and the states that carry meaning

A prop earns its place through its state or its use. Seeds, not a menu; any real object a parent knows can join.

**Kitchen**
- *Mug* — full and gone cold (waited too long); two mugs side by side (company); set down beside someone without a word
  (care); chipped, or mended with visible seams, and still used (repair).
- *Kettle* — both reaching for it at once after a fight (an awkward truce); starting to boil while a parent reads a text
  (anger building).
- *Plate* — untouched (something is wrong); slid across the table (a peace offering); left outside a bedroom door (care
  after conflict).
- *Fridge door with one magnet* — the school letter, a certificate, the bad test pinned beside the good one.
- *Car keys* — on the hook (who is home), in the parent's mitten by the door (rushing), handed over (trust).
- *Lunchbox* — left on the counter (rescue them, or let them learn).
- *A pan* — scrubbed too hard ("I'm fine").
- *Cereal bowl and spoon* — the spoon stopped halfway (the non-answer).
- *Salt* — passed without eye contact (the silence after an argument).

**Living room**
- *TV remote* — who holds it (who is in control); both mittens on it (the tug).
- *Sofa* — the gap between two people on it; sliding closer.
- *A shared blanket* — over both laps.
- *Laptop* — lid half closed (attention split); turned round to show something (inviting in).
- *The parent's phone* — face-down on the sofa arm (attention given) or face-up and buzzing (attention pulled).

**Teenager's bedroom**
- *Headphones* — on (closed), round the neck (available), one side held out (sharing).
- *Charger cable* — stretched from the wall to the bed (night scrolling).
- *The door* — open, ajar, closed, a strip of light under it.
- *A guitar or skateboard leaning in the corner* — who he is.
- *Laundry on the floor* — the chores argument.
- *Desk lamp* — still on at night.

**Hall and front door**
- *School bag* — dropped just inside the door (the day was bad).
- *Keys on the hook* — who is home, who is out late.
- *The front door* — the hug on arrival, the slow late opening, the hall light left on.

**Car**
- *Steering wheel* — the parent's grip tightening; the parent's hand reaching for it from the passenger seat (control).
- *Rear-view mirror* — eyes meeting.
- *The ignition key turned off* — staying in the car to talk.

**School and homework**
- *The test paper* — with the teacher's circle. Red counts as the danger colour: use it only when the grade is the
  threat in the scene.
- *Permission slip* — forgotten, unsigned, handed over at the last moment.
- *School letter in an envelope* — handed over without eye contact.
- *Eraser* — worn down to a stub (perfectionism).
- *Pencil* — the parent taking it out of the child's mitten (help that makes it worse).

**Sport**
- *Kit bag* — by the door, untouched for days (quitting).
- *Water bottle, football* — the forgotten bottle, the missed shot.
- *A medal on its ribbon* — hung on the bedroom door handle (a real object, not a trophy icon).
- *The bench* — sitting it out.

**Phone and online**
- *The phone* — face-down (hiding, or attention given); face-up and buzzing (the pull); a cracked screen (an accident
  to confess); lit in the dark (night scrolling); turned toward the parent (trust). Screens show plain grey message bars
  or one simple drawn picture, never words.
- *The laptop* — the quick tab switch.

**Bedtime**
- *Bedside lamp* — on late.
- *Duvet* — lifted for the child after a nightmare.
- *Alarm clock* — hands only, no numbers.
- *A book* — left open on the bed.

**Money and work**
- *The bill envelope* — its stamp in danger red only when money is the threat.
- *Work laptop open at the dinner table* — work in the family's space.

**Grandparents**
- *Reading glasses, a biscuit tin, a photo album, a teacup and saucer, the armchair* (empty, in a grief story).

**Growing up**
- *Height marks on the door frame* (pencil lines, no numbers); *a box of old toys*; *the first set of house keys*.

## 4. Colour on props

- Each frame names one colour element (`ce`): a prop, one part of a prop, or none (the face carries the frame). "If
  the phone is important, let the phone stand out."
- A recurring hero object keeps one fixed colour all film long, so it is recognised when it returns.
- Everything that is not the colour element is outline: the hero object a bold black outline with white fill;
  secondary objects and set pieces thin soft-grey outlines with white fill.
- Red stays for danger only.
- Prompt phrasing lives in `style-and-colour-v19.md`.

## 5. Metaphors — stronger, not more

**What Thomas asked.** "stronger visual metaphors" (final priorities). His earlier notes (Video 4 review) asked for
bolder objects and metaphors that *amplify* rather than illustrate the voice-over — his examples were a heavy backpack,
a wall between parent and teen, a giant phone pulling the character in, a giant clock, a mountain of messages — and,
after Video 1, for the message to be shown in a surprising way rather than exactly what the voice-over says. He also
said: "The rule is not: every scene needs a metaphor."

**The v19 rule.** A metaphor is used where it adds meaning the words do not already carry. Real moments carry the
everyday lines (`everyday-situations-v19.md`). Love, trust, attention and safety lines are staged as behaviour first
(`acting-and-interaction-v19.md` §8).

### 5.1 Amplify, don't illustrate
- **Amplifies** — the picture adds a twist, an exaggeration or a consequence that is not in the words.
- **Illustrates** — the picture draws the noun or verb the voice-over already says.

R2's audit of "Seven Things" sorted the metaphor families: some amplified, a similar number only illustrated, a few
were mixed (12 / 11 / 5 — a measurement, not a target). The amplifying ones were the most memorable:
- walking on thin ice toward MOM ("disappointing them feels terrifying");
- the door closing a little more each year;
- the height marks on the door frame that evolve into SON's own mark;
- MOM as a breezy judge over her own spill ("You forgive yourself faster");
- adults landing on a cushion, children on a red circle ("Adults receive context. Children receive correction.");
- the gold-seamed mug ("not perfection. It is recovery.");
- the bills put on top of the fridge, out of reach ("You do not have to tell children every adult problem.").

The illustrating ones mostly repeated a word from the line: an umbrella for "weather", an iceberg for "deeper", a
blanket fort for "inside", a magnifier for "detective", a scribble for "imagination".

**Push one step past the noun** (R2 §6):
- "This is deeper than grades." — instead of an iceberg, SON hands over a test and MOM's eyes go to *his face*, not the
  paper.
- "checking the emotional weather" — instead of an umbrella, SON pauses outside the kitchen and listens to how hard the
  cupboard doors are closing.

### 5.2 When a metaphor earns its place
- The line is about something invisible that a real moment cannot show: pressure building, a weight carried, time
  passing, things adding up, a gap growing, what is underneath a behaviour.
- The line makes a comparison ("it's like…") that a picture can sharpen.
- An emotional peak where amplification makes it land.
- A short metaphor as a pattern interrupt — one of Thomas's own interrupt types.

**Not** when a real moment would say it better, when the line is about love, trust, attention or safety, when the
picture would only draw the noun, or when it needs a generic symbol.

### 5.3 Mature metaphors
- Built from real, adult objects — a door, a bag, a mug, a phone, a table, a kettle, a measuring mark, a staircase —
  with scale, physics or an impossible situation doing the work. Never icons.
- **In-world metaphors are often the strongest and always the most mature:** a real object in its real place behaving with extra
  meaning. The kettle starting to boil as MOM reads the message. The bills lifted onto the top of the fridge. The
  bedroom door a little more closed each year. No impossible staging needed.
- **Scale metaphors** use a real object at an impossible size: the school bag he is bent under, the phone taller than a
  door. Fresh each time — the giant phone pulling the parent in was already a Video 4 example, so it returns only if it
  evolves.
- A character is inside the metaphor and reacts to it. A metaphor with nobody in it is a diagram.
- No faces on objects, no eyes on scribbles, no hearts or stars inside the metaphor (the juggling of a heart, a sand
  timer and a stop paddle failed on both counts).
- One idea per frame, readable with the sound off, one colour element.
- It grows out of the story's own objects where possible — the video's spine object or a motif.

**Childish → mature (seeds):**
- a rain cloud over one head (sadness) → his chair at the far end of a table that has stretched far too long;
- a podium (pride) → MOM filming every moment of the match, SON looking at her phone instead of the ball;
- a trophy to polish (reward) → the certificate on the fridge door, the child's own drawing slipping down behind it;
- masks in a mirror (identity) → SON at the bathroom mirror trying faces — serious, cool, smiling — as a short run of
  stills;
- a dim bulb over the head (low motivation) → the phone's battery bar almost empty, SON slumped in the same shape;
- a seesaw between a rulebook and a heart (discipline vs connection) → MOM's one palm raised flat, her other arm round
  his shoulders;
- a cracked heart-shaped vase (hurt) → the mug set down too hard, a crack running up its side;
- a scoreboard of likes (comparison) → a screen of other people's photos, and when it goes dark, SON's own face
  reflected in it;
- a pressure gauge into the red (anger building) → the kettle beginning to boil on the hob behind MOM.

## 6. Recurring objects — the evolve rule

Thomas: every recurring object should "have a reason; evolve with the story; mean something different later; support
the emotional progression. It should not return only because it looks good." The summary he approved: "Recurring
objects: These only come back when they mean something new."

**For every return, write the motif log entry** (MOTIFS in the video data: key, meaning, arc):
1. **Reason** — why it returns here; what in the line calls for it.
2. **What changed** — its new state: opened, cracked, mended, buried, face-down, handed over, smaller, empty.
3. **New meaning** — what it means now that it did not mean before.
4. **Emotional step** — which step of the story's emotional progression it supports.
5. **New picture** — a new shot size, angle and composition. Never the same picture as before.

If a return cannot fill all five, it does not return. The object keeps its design and its colour (so it is recognised);
its state, meaning and picture change.

**From "Seven Things":** the jar's *meaning* evolved well — hidden behind SON's back in a hug, a weight on his back
lifted by one sentence, cracked and mended, buried at night, opened at the kitchen table to tell the truth. But its
*picture* did not: most chapter openers showed it alone, centred, at eye level, on a surface. A new picture each time
would have been the lid in extreme close-up, the jar seen from above, the jar in SON's lap, the jar reflected in an eye.
The door evolved in both meaning and picture: the boy at the door deciding → slammed → MOM and SON back to back
through it → open again.

**Arc entry format (suggested):** `"S45 · state: … · now means: … · step: … · picture: …"`.

## 7. Metaphor seeds by theme — open

Each theme: mature seeds (a real object with a twist) → how it can evolve. Seeds, not a menu.

- **Attention and phones** — the phone growing heavier in MOM's mitten each time the child speaks; MOM's face lit by the
  screen while the child's drawing lowers. *Evolves:* the phone face-down on the table → handed to the teen to show
  something.
- **Listening vs fixing** — MOM opening a toolbox at the sight of SON's sad face; her "solution" mouth open while his
  shoulders sink. *Evolves:* the toolbox shut and pushed aside; MOM sitting down beside him.
- **Pressure and stress** — the school bag that gets heavier as each activity is named, SON bent under it; the kettle
  boiling while the parent reads the bill. *Evolves:* the bag set down and emptied one thing at a time.
- **Comparison** — the height marks on the door frame with other people's marks above his; the sibling's certificate on
  the fridge covering his drawing. *Evolves:* his own mark, at his own height, with nobody else's beside it.
- **Shame and hiding** — the test pushed deeper into the bag; the cracked phone kept face-down in his lap; a drawer that
  will not quite close. *Evolves:* the drawer opened, the thing put on the table.
- **Trust and honesty** — the house key handed over; the phone screen turned toward the parent. *Evolves:* the bedroom
  door from locked, to ajar, to open.
- **Control and autonomy** — MOM's hand reaching for the steering wheel from the passenger seat; a parent still holding
  the bike seat long after the child can ride. *Evolves:* the hand letting go.
- **Repair and forgiveness** — the mended mug with visible seams (no heart on it); the plate slid across the table
  after the fight. *Evolves:* the mended mug used again, handed to the child.
- **Time and growing up** — the door closing a little more each year; the small mitten in MOM's hand at the gate, then
  a gap between them. *Evolves:* the grown teen holding the door open for her.
- **Overwhelm** — the laundry pile rising to the chin; a list stuck to the fridge that grows down over the whole door
  (lines, no words). *Evolves:* one thing taken off at a time.
- **Distance and disconnection** — the table that stretches longer between them; a wall built across the kitchen (Video
  4's example, only if it evolves). *Evolves:* the table back to its size, the chairs pulled together.
- **Fear of the reaction** — walking on thin ice toward the parent; listening at the kitchen door to how hard the
  cupboards close. *Evolves:* he walks in without stopping to listen.
- **Approval** — a stage light that comes on only after the win. *Evolves:* the light that stays on when he fails.
- **Safety** — the hall light left on for him at night. *Evolves:* he leaves it on for her.
- **Anger** — the kettle, the slammed door, the dent in the car door. *Evolves:* parent and teen fixing the dent together, side by side.

## 8. Method: invent a new metaphor

1. **Name the idea and the feeling** in the line.
2. **Ask first: is there a real moment that shows it?** If yes, use the real moment.
3. **If the idea is invisible, choose a physical lens** that carries the feeling: weight, size, distance, containment,
   temperature, pressure, time, connection, light, growth and decay, balance, direction (`metaphor-bank.md` §1).
4. **Find the real, adult object** in the story's own world that can carry that lens — the bag, the door, the kettle,
   the phone, the table — ideally the spine object or a motif.
5. **Push it past the noun.** What twist, exaggeration or consequence does the picture add that the words do not say?
6. **Put a character inside it, reacting.**
7. **Plan its change** as an image edit (it grows, closes, cracks, lifts) and decide whether it returns. If it returns,
   plan the evolution (§6).
8. **Test it:** mature (§1); amplifies, not illustrates (§5.1); readable with the sound off; one idea, not a pile of
   props; one colour element.
9. **Record it:** `idea`, the other ideas in `alt`, the motif entry. After the video, add it to §7.
